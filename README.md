# Hoops Career

A mobile-first basketball GM **and** head-coach game, built as an installable offline PWA with Capacitor shells for Android and iOS. It starts in the 2026-27 season with real rosters (New League also offers a fictional league with the real franchises), and its main feature is a tactics layer that tells you _why_ things happen.

## What's in the box

**Possession-by-possession engine** (`src/core/sim/engine.ts`)

- Every half-court trip runs a concrete action: pick-and-roll, isolation, post-up, handoffs/off-ball screens, or motion/cuts/drive-and-kick. Transition, putbacks, presses and end-of-game situations are modeled too.
- Defense chooses its pick-and-roll coverage (drop, switch, hedge, blitz, ice), base scheme (man, 2-3, 3-2), help level, ball pressure, physicality, transition defense, press and a star plan (deny or double). The 3-2 zone contests threes harder than man-to-man but is softer in the paint, post, cuts and glass, so it beats the 2-3 against shooting teams and loses to post-up and drive teams; switching off the ball denies handoffs, but switched handoffs and cross-screens hand out mismatch isolations and post-ups where the smaller defender fouls. A box-and-one (four in a 2-2 zone, one chaser) or triangle-and-two (three in a zone, two chasers) face-guards their main creators: fewer touches, late catches, a defense that loads up on him sooner, few catch-and-shoot looks and a tiring chase, while a defender caught high gives up drives on the catch and back-cuts, and everyone else gets more open threes, gaps and offensive boards. Named for players in a game plan or live, it plays man-to-man while they sit. Against a star with non-shooters around him a named box-and-one is worth about +1.1 points per 100 possessions; against a shooting team about −1.2.
- Each tactical effect is tagged with its expected-points value (e.g. "drop coverage conceded pull-ups: +3.4"). That is what drives the post-game report, live assistant tips and the Tactics Lab.
- Rotations follow a minute-by-minute stint plan built from your depth chart. The staff overrides it for fouls, fatigue, injuries and blowouts (hard minute caps hold: a capped player sits down up to 40 seconds early, since subs wait for a dead ball), and playoff rotations shorten to about eight (the starters and closers always among them). The starters open both halves, and the closers finish the game; the coach's starting and closing fives take the floor whatever their positions. A fouled-out or injured player is replaced by whoever the staff wants on the floor at that point, so he isn't subbed straight back out. A reserve who starts for an injured or resting starter takes over that starter's minutes. Garbage time empties the bench in real blowouts only: the starters come straight back if the game gets within reach, and nobody subs in a whole new five in the last half minute. You can also sub manually during live games.
- NBA rules at the edges of the game: teams get a shot off before the buzzer (a team tied or down three or less gets one off with a second or two left; a team protecting a late 4th-quarter lead runs out the clock instead), the shot clock runs from the inbound (breaking a press uses some of it) and a side-out foul restarts it at 14 or more, a timeout stops play (the trip out of it is an inbound, never a run-out) and in a live ball only the team with the ball can call one, hack-a-player stops in the last two minutes of every period, trailing teams foul only while a foul can still buy another possession, at most two timeouts after the 3:00 mark of the 4th, and short-handed teams never play four (a player with six fouls stays in when nobody is left, and every further foul is a technical; an injured player with no healthy sub is replaced by the last player disqualified). Box scores add up to exactly 48 minutes a team (plus 5 per overtime), and the starters are the five who took the floor for the tip.
- AI coaches adjust during games: at quarter breaks and timeouts, how often they change coverage, help, pressure or the glass depends on their adjustments rating. Your assistant can do the same in your simmed games (Game Mgmt), and game reports list both benches' adjustments by quarter.
- Calibrated to 2025-26 league averages: pace ≈ 100, ORtg ≈ 114, ~41% of shots from three, about 27 assists per team game with NBA-like assisted shares, and turnover, rebounding, foul and block rates within 5%. Games vary like the NBA's (a margin SD near 13 for a given matchup, about 16% decided by 20+, about 4% to overtime, the two scores moving together), and team quality spreads the standings: team net ratings track what the players' real box plus-minus (BPM) predicts at the minutes they get (r ≈ 0.86), and win SD is about 11 (the NBA's is about 12). Tactic extremes have real costs, so no slider setting is a free win. A full season sims in about 8 s on a laptop.

**Coaching & understanding**

- **Tactics**: playbooks with live "fit" scores for your roster, every 0-100 setting picked from five named levels (pace: Grind it out, Patient, Balanced, Up-tempo, Run & gun; each action from Almost never to Heavily) and explained in place, and Simple/Advanced/Expert depth. Expert mode adds roles, shot freedom, screeners and late-game rules. A fit is a starting score plus what each roster quality adds or takes away (lead creator, shooting, a passing big, rim protection, depth...), and tapping it opens "Why fit 72": the pluses and minuses, the players behind each and what would raise it. The header says "Custom (Balanced base)" once the settings leave a preset. Presets, the staff's in-game adjustments and situational plans can land between the levels: the level nearest the value is the one shown, and the engine plays the value itself.
- **Situational plans and crunch time**: up to four situational plans (Tactics → Game Mgmt in Expert; Advanced gets Protect a lead and Chasing late as switches) change your tactics while a situation lasts (a quarter range, time left, the score, or the second unit on the floor): the first enabled plan that matches plays on top of your base tactics until the situation ends, and the play-by-play says so. Live edits always go to the base tactics. A go-to player gets the ball much more in the last two minutes of a close fourth quarter or overtime, and an after-timeout play opens your next half-court trip after your timeouts. In the last two minutes of a close live game a Late game card calls plays from the bench (go for three, quick two, run the clock, an isolation, foul now, with a timeout first if wanted); a call changes what the team looks for, never how well it executes. Game plans can name hack-a-player targets and the quarter hacking starts, and a player hacked twice in a period goes to the bench when a reserve his size can come in. Expert's Foul to give fouls the ball handler at once when the other team has the ball with 3 to 8 seconds left in a period and a foul is only a side out (in the 4th and overtime only when tied or up by three or less), so they inbound with less time and a worse first look; about one in twenty is called on the shot. AI teams use none of these, so the league's numbers are unchanged.
- **Sets and play calls** (`src/core/sim/sets.ts`): your offense can run seven named sets: Spain pick-and-roll, Horns, Floppy, Elevator doors, Hammer, Chicago and Post split. Each is run for the player on the floor who fits it best (or one you name) and goes for its own look: the roll, an elbow touch or a pop, a curl or a catch-and-shoot three, the corner kick, the split cut. Nothing in a set makes anyone shoot better: each has edges against the defenses it's built to beat (Spain against drop and hedge, Floppy and Chicago against fighting over screens, Hammer against heavy help) and none, or worse, against the ones that kill it (switching, zones, staying home), and takes a second or two more of the shot clock. Defenses read sets: each run in a game makes its look a little less open and its passes easier to jump, sooner against coaches rated high at tactics, so a set is strongest early (a first run is worth about +0.1 points per possession against the defense it beats; each read costs about 0.05). Tactics → Offense keeps a package of up to four that your half court mixes in on fresh trips (never in late-game situations, only for players who fit, and your staff stops running one once the defense is on it); any set can be your after-timeout play, run for a player you name; and live, the Plays control (Advanced) calls a set or a play for the next half-court trip at any time, shows it as pending until it's run, and says how far the defense has read each set. A package is a small edge, not a free one: about +0.3 points per 100 possessions at best in the Lab, a little negative against the wrong defense. Game and period reports list each set's runs, points per possession and the run that got it read, and the Lab's Compare can test a package. AI coaches run no sets, so their games play exactly as before.
- **Saved playbooks** (`src/core/sim/playbooks.ts`): "My playbooks" keeps up to twelve named playbooks of your own (schemes, every slider, your set package, game management, and roles, shot freedom and screeners by player, which only load for players still on your roster; matchups, star, chase and hunt targets and instructions by player are game by game and aren't saved). Each shows what loading it would change ("Pace 62 → 80", "Coverage Drop → Hedge") and can be loaded, overwritten, renamed, deleted or compared with your current tactics in the Lab; the header names the playbook your tactics came from, with "· edited" once they move from it. The library is the coach's, so it comes along when you take over another team.
- **Game plans** (`src/core/league/gamePlans.ts`, `/gameplan/:tid`): a plan for each opponent, for the next game against them, the current playoff series or the rest of the season. Its items replace one another by kind (one ball-screen coverage, one star plan...) and change actions relative to your playbook, so they build on whatever you run; matchups can be planned too. The Game Plan screen (from Tactics' Upcoming opponents card, a team's Scouting tab, the Lab, the preview or Home) sets the scope, lists the items with the evidence behind them and the assistant's untested counters to add, sets a star plan on any of their top eight, and from Advanced depth plans matchups: their projected top eight against your top ten, with the staff's tip-off assignment (from the engine's own matchup solver, `src/core/sim/matchups.ts`) and what each pairing means (size gap, his creation against your defender's perimeter D). From Advanced depth a plan can also set a junk defense and who it chases, how to guard each of their top eight (`src/core/analysis/guarding.ts`: go under screens, top-lock him off the line, sag off him, get physical; each changes only the actions he's in, with the staff's ★ suggestion from his games or his ratings), and which of their defenders to attack (your isolations, post-ups and ball screens go at the man he guards, which costs some ball movement; a coach being hunted with success hides him on your weakest scorer). The preview's Key matchups and the live player sheet set the same instructions, and none of it is kept beyond that opponent. The assistant suggests a box-and-one or triangle-and-two against a one- or two-man offense whose others can't shoot, and attacking a real weak link, with the numbers.
- **Scouting from the games played** (`src/core/analysis/scouting.ts`): each team's Scouting tab reads its actual games: pace, offensive and defensive ratings, the four factors at both ends, three-point and rim rates with league ranks, play types at both ends with points per possession against the league's (and how unusual each is), key players with on/off numbers and your meetings with them, read into plain-English tendencies and ways to attack them (last season's numbers, labelled, until a team has played five games, without play types: of last season's results the save keeps only your games). The assistant's counters are drafted against the plan you already have for that opponent, and those the numbers back carry that evidence and come first (sprint back against a transition team, defend without fouling against a team that lives at the line, press after makes against loose handlers, post-ups against a weak post defense...).
- **Tactics Lab**: runs real engine games in a Web Worker. _Project_ your plan against any opponent, _Compare_ before vs after your edits (each possession replays from the same random stream, so the difference is the tactics; verdicts need two standard errors), or let the assistant generate _Counters_ and test each one (the verdict range widens with the number of counters tested, so one that changes nothing rarely looks like a win; each is tested as the game plan would play it, in place of the plan's item of the same kind). A saved playbook can be the Compare baseline. In the play-in and playoffs the Lab plays those games, with the assistants' rotations shortened to eight as in the real ones.
- **Opposing game plans** (`src/core/ai/opponentPlan.ts`): AI coaches scout you too. On normal a coach comes in with a plan for about half your games (more often the better he is at tactics), on hard every coach does, and on casual none do. A plan is up to two changes to his usual tactics, each answering something your games show (a hedge or ice against a pick-and-roll you score well from, a drop against hot rollers when he has a rim protector, staying home on a three-point team or packing the paint against one that can't shoot, sprinting back, defending without fouling, ball pressure on loose handlers, running against a team that doesn't get back, denying or doubling the player you run your offense through, or a box-and-one on him when the rest of your team doesn't make its threes) or exposes something in yours (attacking your weakest perimeter defender when he gives up a lot to the rest of your rotation, or guarding one of your top seven the way your own staff would read him: going under on a handler who can't pull up, top-locking a shooter who can't drive, sagging off a non-shooter, getting physical with a poor free-throw shooter inside). The preview, the opponent's Scouting tab and the Game Plan screen say "Expect from them tonight: ices ball screens, denies Brunson the ball" (with the numbers behind each item from Advanced depth), the preview and the Lab play their plan exactly as the game will, the live period report lists it, and the report's Why tab keeps "Their game plan". Whether he plans, and what, comes from the game's id and your games before game day, so it never touches the league's random stream and AI-vs-AI games are unchanged.
- **Pre-game** (`/preview`, `src/core/analysis/preview.ts`): Home's Watch & coach opens a matchup preview first (Settings → Pre-game screen turns this off; the Play menu's Watch & coach live always goes straight to the game). It shows both teams' records, conference ranks, form and ratings with league ranks, back-to-backs and playoff stakes ("R1 · Game 5", elimination games); a projection (this season's net ratings, weighted toward what each rotation is worth tonight until enough games are in, plus about 1.3 points of home court) that "Simulate 40 games" checks with real engine games at tonight's venue; both injury reports (out, resting, worn down) and both projected starting fives as the game will start them; the opponent's play types against the league's, its defense and how often its coach adjusts; who guards their key players at the tip, with a picker that plans the matchup; and your game plan for them, with the assistant's counters one tap away. Storylines (`src/core/analysis/storylines.ts`) set up the night: a player facing a team he played for ("for the first time since he left" only when every game since is stored), a career milestone one good night away, team streaks and a player's run of 20-point nights, last season's playoff rematch, the MVP race and two No. 1 picks. The injury report makes the game-time decisions: a player one game from back from a minor or moderate injury (not a concussion) is questionable and sits unless you clear him, and cleared he plays about 6% below his best and twice as likely to get hurt in the game, with the game counting as the one he had left (the starters, the projection, the Lab and a resumed live game all play it that way; other teams always sit theirs). Home's next-game card counts down to tip-off and lists who is out (or questionable) on both sides, the opponent's form, the season series and the night's lead storyline, and the Play menu names the opponent ("vs BOS (12-8) · today"). Keys to the game (`src/core/analysis/keys.ts`) give you three numbers to hit tonight, read from both teams' games before today: what they do best to take away (keep them off the offensive glass, run them off the three-point line, defend without fouling, get back in transition, protect the paint), what their defense does to teams (take care of the ball against one that forces turnovers; crash the glass, get to the line or let it fly against one that gives those up), making the scorer they run it through work, and sharing the ball. Each is the strongest signals first (standard deviations from the league's teams, never two on one number) and each target is about where tonight's number lands if both teams play to form (their own and what the other allows, halved), so a little under half are met (the offensive glass is read as a share of the misses, since a count grows with a team's own misses); over three teams' seasons, the games with none met were won 31% of the time, one 40%, two 63% and all three 78%. In a later season's first five games they read last season's team totals (its results keep only your games); on a new league's opening nights, with no games to read, they come from the other coach's tactics and his players' last season. Because they read only the games before tonight, the preview, the live game and the recorded result all have the same three.
- **Live game**: an animated 2D court driven by the engine's possession logs, plus timeouts, subs and in-game tactic changes. Every screen keeps the score, the court and the coaching buttons in view at once: on a phone the scorebug and the court stay pinned at the top while the strip, the assistant and the play-by-play scroll under them, and a coaching dock (TO with the timeouts left, Subs, Plays, Adjust, Box) takes the tab bar's place until the final; a tablet held sideways puts the scorebug and a court sized to the screen's height on the left and the dock and the strip at the top of the right; held upright it stacks like a phone; a phone held sideways gives the court the left side and pins the scorebug at the top of the right. Playback sits over the court like a video's: pause, the speed (1×/2×/4×), Key plays and Skip, and Replay while paused. The court calls each play by name as it draws it (`captionAt` in `src/ui/live/choreo.ts`: "Holmgren screens for Mitchell: they drop", "Mitchell finds Williams", "Williams, catch-and-shoot three…", "good! (Mitchell assist)", "Blocked by Wembanyama!", "And one! Foul on Castle"), with the engine's own shot calls (the step-back three, the alley-oop, the putback dunk the play-by-play names) and, in the last two minutes of the fourth or overtime, a make that ties it or takes the lead ("OKC lead 101-100!"); the ball handler wears a name tag, the team with the ball is named on the baseline it attacks, a shot leaves a trail, and the net ripples on a make and the rim rattles on a miss (none of the motion with reduced motion on). A make as the clock runs out flashes BUZZER BEATER, or GAME WINNER from behind or level in the fourth or overtime, and plays again once in slow motion (Settings → Instant replays), felt on a phone; any play can be replayed from the pause button, and the possession it interrupted picks up where it was. A game from its tip opens with the starting lineups on the court (the arena, what's at stake, both fives with their numbers, positions and season lines) until you tip it off or a few seconds pass (Settings → Starting lineups at tip-off). Around the league (`src/ui/live/scoreboard.ts`, Settings → Scores around the league): the night's other games tick along with yours on a bottom line under the scorebug, each tipping at its home team's local 7:00 or 7:30 and running on the same broadcast clock (breaks and a fifteen-minute halftime included), read from its quarters, since the day's other games are played before yours; the ones that matter come first (teams within three places of yours once ten games are in, your next opponent, every playoff and play-in game), one that matters comes up as it goes final, and a tap opens every score. Everything on screen (scorebug, strip, alerts, box score, play-by-play) shows the game as the court has drawn it, never the possession still being drawn. Win probability (`src/core/analysis/winProb.ts`) rides under the scorebug (Settings → Win probability in live games): the chance to win from the score, the clock, who has the ball and the pre-game projection. It treats what's left as a normal margin, with the share of a lead engine games give back early (about 15% with 19 minutes or more left) and a spread that never quite closes while time is left, and is fitted on engine games (on 1,231 held-out games: log loss 0.459 against 0.501 for the margin alone, every 10% bucket within 2 points); a game in progress never reads 0% or 100%. Broadcast graphics go up over the backcourt now and then (a big quarter, one man scoring his team's last nine, perfect shooting, 30/40/50-point nights, a double-double or triple-double, a new season high or one within reach late, a career milestone, a franchise or league scoring record from the record book or one within reach late, a lopsided team number at the breaks): at most one every two game minutes, each once, only the big ones in the last 24 seconds of a period, and only what the drawn game shows. Under the court a strip shows both fives with energy, fouls, points and who's hot ("4 straight makes") or cold: tap one of yours for his sheet (sub him out, his shot freedom tonight, his role, go-to in crunch time, a set run for him and who he guards, by coaching depth), or one of theirs for his free throws, a Hack-a and how to guard him (deny, double, go under, top-lock, sag off, body up, or attack him). The scorebug adds team fouls (lit in the penalty), runs and the playoff series or Cup stage; each timeout flashes once, naming the team, and banners report injuries, foul-outs and the other coach's adjustments. The assistant speaks up only when the staff won't handle it (tired legs and foul trouble when you make the subs or in crunch time, 5 fouls late with a Sit him button, a run the staff won't stop, with honest copy: a timeout rests legs and resets the run, and gives their coach a chance to adjust), a play type running hot comes with a counter ("drop is giving their handlers room", Switch to Hedge), and the biggest tactical edge against you comes with a one-tap fix. Quick pills (Push pace, Zone 2-3, Pack the paint, Press after makes, Deny or Double their hot scorer, Hack-a, the late-game fouls) follow your coaching depth, as does the Adjust sheet (with a Late game section in Expert), and Reset to game plan goes back to tip-off. Calling a timeout opens a huddle: the score, the clock and your chance to win, who's tired or in foul trouble (one tap to sub), the assistant's top fix, and a play to draw up for the trip out of it. One called while they have a live ball (after a steal or a defensive board) is taken at the next dead ball, and none can be called at a period's buzzer; a play called then, at the break, is for the first trips of the next period. "Keep changes after this game" also keeps what you changed before turning it on, and never saves tonight's game plan or matchups. Games stop at every quarter, at halftime only, or never (Settings), with a period report: score by quarter, the keys to the game so far (each one's number, on pace for a full game at the rate so far, and On track, Behind, Done or Missed; the huddle shows them too), team comparison, who's scoring, foul and energy trouble, what's deciding it with Apply buttons, and their play types running hot; a tie after regulation breaks as Overtime. Skip ahead goes to the end of the quarter, halftime, clutch time (5:00 left in the fourth) or the final; the box score has Team, Flow (with the win-probability line) and Shots tabs (by team, period and shooter), and the period report states the win probability; Key plays mode draws only crunch time, the ends of periods, runs, timeouts, anything the assistant flags and plays that put up a graphic, and fast-forwards the rest. The speed you pick sticks. The play-by-play shows everything, the scoring plays or one team's. A live game survives the app being closed: it's journaled in the save (`League.live`: how it tipped off and every sub, tactics change, timeout and play call, with the possession it came after), brought up to date at breaks, on pause, with every move and when the app is hidden, and played back on reopening ("Picked up where you left off: Q3 4:12"). The engine draws every possession from the game's seed and possession counts, so the replay is the same game and must land on the recorded score and clock; a journal that doesn't is dropped and the game starts again from tip-off, with a note. Until the game is played, the Play menu, Home, the pre-game screen and the front office (Inbox trade offers included) treat it as a game in progress.
- **Post-game recap** (on the live final card and at the top of each of your reports): how the keys to the game went (kept with the result: "2 of 3", each with its number on the night; a scorer who sat out doesn't count); the three stars (the player of the game first, from both teams); win probability's turning point (the play that swung it most the winner's way, named from the play-by-play), a comeback ("won after falling to 6%") or a lead let slip; round career thousands reached that night; your conference rank and games back before and after (kept with the result); newly clinched spots, your streak or the series and its next game; quotes from your player and the other coach (`src/core/analysis/quotes.ts`: templates for the margin, overtime, big nights, streaks, upsets, comebacks and collapses, injuries and what decided it, picked by the game's id so a game always reads the same); the night's results that matter to you; and your next game. After your latest game you can hold an optional press conference: credit the group, praise the player of the game, or challenge the effort after a loss, each nudging your players' morale a little, once per game. The live final card plays the night's highlights on the court (`src/core/analysis/highlights.ts`: up to eight plays, at most three a period, dunks, threes, and-ones, blocks and steals worth more late in a close game and the shot that won it most of all, yours counted 30% more but their big ones in it too, in the order they happened, each with when it happened and the score it left), and the save keeps the latest live game's reel (`League.reel`, about 10 KB) so its game report can play it until the next live game.
- **Post-game "Why"**: the ranked factors that decided the game, with point values and advice (advice is only given when it still applies to your current tactics, and names the controls as the Tactics screen does). Also four factors, play-type PPP, your sets (runs, points per possession, the run that got each read), lineups, player grades, shot charts, score flow with win probability (your team above, the three biggest swings marked and named, from the projection the game kept at tip-off), momentum swings (when, and from what score) and play-by-play by period.
- **Rotation**: reorderable depth chart, minute targets, caps, closers, rest on back-to-backs, and a 48-minute stint-plan visual.
- **Lineup lab** (`/lineups`, "See all" on the Rotation screen's lineups card, `src/core/analysis/lineups.ts`): your five-man units, the trios and pairs inside them (every five-man stint counts for each) and each player's on/off split, this season or last, sorted by minutes, net, ORtg or DRtg, with minimum-minutes chips, a player filter and rows under 100 possessions faded. Any five can become your starters (swapping depth-chart spots and minutes, as on the Rotation screen) or your closers. Simple coaching depth shows five-man units and on/off with minutes and net only.
- **Player comparison** (`/compare?a=…&b=…`, `src/ui/compare.ts`): two players side by side, from Compare on any player's page (a picker that searches every player and free agent, this year's prospects too in the draft phases, with quick picks: his teammates at his position, yours there and the players in this device's recent comparisons), from a free agent's offer sheet (against your best player at his position) or a prospect's card on the big board (against the next one on it). Each column shows who he is (team, position, age, height, OVR and potential, archetype, salary and years left or a free agent's asking price, injury), then rows for the scouting profile (the player page's percentiles, as bars growing from the middle), the ratings by group, this season's per-game and advanced line (his latest season before his first game), career totals and highs, and honors, the better side of each row lit: fewer turnovers and fouls and the younger player (not against a retired one) are better, games played, size, usage and three-point attempts aren't judged, two values that read the same are a tie and a missing one ("—") decides nothing, except in the honors, where "—" is none and counts as zero. Either side can be changed or the two swapped, and a player no longer in the save leaves an empty column to pick into.
- **Advanced stats** (`src/core/analysis/advanced.ts`): seasons played in this league get the numbers Basketball Reference works out from the box score: PER (Hollinger's, pace-adjusted and normalized to the league's 15), win shares (offense from Dean Oliver's points produced, defense from his individual defensive rating, against the league's points per possession and its points per win, with the box score's possession estimate) and WS/48, and the assist, rebound, steal, block, turnover and usage rates. Over a simulated season PER averages exactly 15 over the league's minutes and the teams' win shares come within about 1% of their wins (2.8 wins a team, RMS), with leaders that read like an NBA season's (Jokić 32.9 PER, .335 WS/48). They show next to imported seasons' own on the player page, in the League hub's Stats tab (columns, and Win shares and PER leaders) and in Compare. Box plus-minus isn't computed, so BPM, VORP and EPM stay the imported seasons'; and only the regular season, since the teams' playoff totals aren't kept.

**League systems**

- **Calendar**: the real 2026-27 schedule (82 games, 41 at home; a Cup quarterfinal or semifinal host plays one more at home, its opponent one fewer); later seasons and fictional leagues get a generated NBA-format one with no four games in five nights, no games on Thanksgiving or Christmas Eve, the All-Star Game on a Sunday and the trade deadline the Thursday ten days before it. Playoff games are every other day with breaks between rounds, so the Finals end in June; the summer has dates too (the draft in late June, free agency from June 30).
- **Competition**: the NBA Cup, standings with NBA tiebreakers (multi-team ties broken as a group; once the regular season is over every seed is marked), play-in and 2-2-1-1-1 playoffs. The news log keeps about a year of results, awards and transactions; injuries make the news from 10 games out (5 for a star). Career milestones make the news too (every 5,000 points; 5,000 rebounds or assists and every 2,500 after), counted only for careers the game has whole: a real veteran's three seasons on record aren't his career, so his aren't claimed.
- **Season forecast** (`src/core/analysis/odds.ts`, League → Standings → Odds): the rest of the season is played out 1,000 times, game by game on the real schedule, then the play-in, the 2-2-1-1-1 playoffs and the lottery's drawing, from each team's strength as the pre-game projection reads it (this season's net rating regressed toward what its roster is worth, an injured player counted for the games he'll be back for), itself uncertain (by 2.5 points per 100 before a game is played, less as the games come in). Each team gets its projected record with the 10th-90th percentile of wins and its chances of the No. 1 seed, the top six, the play-in, the playoffs, each round and the title; the Lottery view gives each team's own first-round pick its chances at No. 1 and the top four and where it lands on average (with its owner when it was traded). It reads only the league, each simulated season on a stream of its own seeded from the league and the day, so every screen shows the same forecast and the league's draws never change; 100% and "—" mean clinched or out (as the standings' clinch marks say), anything else rounds to >99% or <1%. Home and every team page carry the team's line ("Projected 48-34 · Playoff odds 72% (+8 this week) · Title 3%", in the playoffs "Series vs BOS: 58% to win it"), the pre-game screen gives a regular-season game's stakes ("Playoff odds with a win 71% · with a loss 58%", from the same simulated seasons), and the user's playoff and title odds are kept a point a week (`League.oddsTrail`, under 0.5 KB) for a chart on the Odds view. Over twelve simulated seasons (forecasts from opening night to day 140), teams given 10-25% made the playoffs 18% of the time, 25-40% 39%, 60-75% 66% and 75-90% 84% (a Brier score of 0.113); a midseason forecast takes about 0.1 s on a laptop.
- **Honors**: All-Star selection by the NBA format (at least four guards and six frontcourt players per conference) and game, full awards (MVP, DPOY, ROY, 6MOY, MIP, COY, All-NBA, All-Defense, All-Rookie, Finals MVP; the 65-game rule, 50 games for Sixth Man, Most Improved only for players with last season's numbers to improve on, and the League hub's award ladders use the same rules as the vote), history and Hall of Fame. Franchises retire their legends' numbers (`src/core/league/jerseys.ts`): when a player retires, each team he gave five seasons (a seeded player's real seasons count) and a career it remembers raises the number he wore there: his main team for a Hall of Famer, or any with an MVP or Finals MVP, a title with two All-NBA selections, three All-NBA or five All-Star selections there, or its scoring record in this league (15,000 points at least). None go up in a league's first four seasons, about one a season through its twelfth, then three or four a season as the stars it opened with retire; each is in the news (and the GM's inbox for his own team) and hangs as a banner in History's Franchise view and on the team page's History tab. A player already out when the coaches vote (about seven games before the game) who won't be back for it isn't picked, and an All-Star hurt after the vote is replaced only when the injury keeps him out more than two games. The Rising Stars game opens that day (`src/core/league/risingStars.ts`): the ten best rookies against the ten best sophomores (by game score a game and OVR, with 30% of the busiest team's games played; no game without eight a side), an engine exhibition on a stream of its own that counts in no stats, its rosters, box lines, score and MVP on the Awards tab (a "Rising Stars MVP" chip, a token like the contests), the news and, when your players are in it, the inbox. All-Star Saturday (`src/core/league/allStarSaturday.ts`) comes next. The Three-Point Contest invites eight healthy shooters by threes made and percentage this season (2.5 attempts a team game at least; a couple of All-Stars when they're among the twenty best) to the NBA's 27-ball round (five racks of four and a money ball, one rack all money balls at his favorite spot, two deep threes: 40 points at most), the top three to a final and ties to a shoot-off rack; each shot's chance comes from his three-point rating and a little of his form, money balls and deep shots harder and legs tiring late in a round, so first rounds average about 21 and the winning final about 26 (21-31 for 80% of winners). The Slam Dunk Contest invites four young high-flyers who aren't stars (26 or younger first, by bounce, speed, finishing and size; no All-Stars, nobody 85+): two dunks a round, five judges' 6-10s each (30-50) from his bounce, the size of the dunk and the crowd, one in ten not finished (30-36), the top two to a final and ties to a dunk-off, each dunk named for its score ("a 360 windmill", "a dunk from the free-throw line" for a 50). The day draws on a random stream of its own, so it never changes a game. The Awards tab shows both contests round by round (every dunk with its score and name), History lists the winners, and the winners get a chip after All-Star MVP (a token in the honours that rank careers, nothing toward the Hall of Fame), the news, and the inbox when one of your players took part. History → Coaches keeps every head coach the league has had (`League.coaches`, about 150 bytes a coach: his profile and his jobs, about 7.5 KB eight seasons in): the user's résumé job by job (seasons, W-L, playoffs, titles), the coaches in charge (seasons with the team, career W-L and win%, titles, Coaches of the Year) and the former ones, available or retired, each a tap from his jobs season by season. A coach's record is read from his teams' stored seasons: a job taken over mid-season counts only the games from that day (and one left mid-season only those before), and a season's playoff run goes to whoever had the team when its season was over. Coach of the Year goes to the coach it named (by his name then), or to whoever had the team at the end of the regular season. The team page's coaching staff card says "In charge since 2027-28 · 120-126 · 1 title" and names the coaches before him; a save from before the registry starts it from the coaches in charge (the user from his current job's first season).
- **Record book** (`src/core/league/records.ts`, History → Records): the best single games since the league began, by a player (points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, threes) and by a team (points, the biggest win, threes), the league's top ten (five in the playoffs) and each franchise's top three (its best in the playoffs), kept as each game is recorded (results older than last season are pruned, so they couldn't be read back later; about 40 KB in all, with a player's name written in only once he leaves the save). The regular season (Cup group games, quarterfinals and semifinals included) and the postseason (play-in and playoffs) keep separate books; the Cup championship and the All-Star Game count in neither, as in the stats, and a record tied stays with whoever set it first. Single-season records (points, rebounds, assists, steals and blocks per game with the NBA's 70%-of-games rule, total points and threes, and the shooting percentages with 300 field goals, 82 threes or 125 free throws made) and career leaders come from the stats every player keeps for good (a retiree whose season stands in those lists is never compacted away), and team seasons (best and worst records, net, offensive and defensive ratings, points per game) from the teams' finished seasons: only seasons played in this league count, never a seeded player's real ones. Each player's page has his career highs (labelled "since 2026-27" unless his whole career is in the book; about 0.15 KB a player). Once the book has a season behind it, league records, a franchise's scoring record of 45 or more and your team's main records make the news (routine news, pruned before trades and signings, since the book keeps them), your team's reach your inbox too, and the post-game recap names the records a game holds. A save from before the book reads it back from this season's results, the only ones it still has whole, and the book starts there. A game's records are its best marks against each list as it stood before the game, and a list with nothing on it yet (a franchise's first postseason) only starts.
- **Player movement**: the 2019-odds draft lottery (teams on the same record split the odds of the places they share, and pick in the reverse of their tie-break draw in the second round), generated draft classes with combine data, a mock draft (`src/core/league/mockDraft.ts`, the Draft screen's Mock view: the draft played out 250 times the way AI teams pick, on a stream of its own, in the order by record until the lottery and the real one after it, each pick named once with how often he was the pick and the next likeliest; the user's picks follow his big board or his staff, and each prospect on the board gets his range, where he went in four of five mocks, with his chance to be gone before the user's next pick; over five simulated drafts the real pick fell inside that range 82% of the time), re-signing and extensions, a 30-day free agency with competing offers for the top free agents, contract talks with counters and restricted free agency (qualifying offers, offer sheets and matching), 10-day contracts, waivers, buyouts and stretched releases, and trades (validation, AI valuation with its reasons, counter-offers, a trade finder, "what would make this work?", AI offers you can accept, draft-day trades, and terms beyond players and picks: protections you set, pick swaps and cash).
- **Players**: morale and role expectations, injuries and load management (worn-down players get hurt more; assistant-run playbooks refit when a long injury starts or ends), and development (players peak around 26-27 and fade faster past 35; training focus trades skills without costing OVR; coaching, practice intensity and minutes). Every player on a team wears his own number (an arrival whose number is taken, or retired there, picks a free one as he joins; a player wearing a number when it's retired keeps it until he leaves), no two active players share a name, and no two head coaches do. Meetings (`src/core/league/morale.ts`): one of your players who asked for a trade, or is unhappy, can be met from his page, the Front Office contract sheet or the trade-request mail, once every 14 days and never while a promise is open (nor, after the season, a player whose contract is up: he's re-signed or let go). Promise a bigger role (the request goes on hold while you play him the minutes he expects, and give a creator the ball, over his next 15 healthy games: kept, he drops it and cheers up; broken, he wants out again, angrier, and takes no promise for the rest of the season), promise a trade (the request stands but he weighs on the locker room half as much; unmoved by the deadline, or by the next opening night after it, he goes public), or ask for patience (likelier the longer he has been with you, the better your record and the older he is, with the odds shown; agreeing puts the request on hold for 20 games, refusing costs mood). AI teams' players don't get promises.
- **Generated players** (draft classes and fictional leagues) get hidden tendencies like the real players' calibrated ones, drawn from how those relate to level and size, and every player's tendencies follow his level each offseason (a draftee who becomes a star takes a star's shots; an aging star hands them on), while a real player's calibrated usage settles toward his level's as his teammates change, so a long career keeps the real league's shot diet and spread: twelve seasons in, about 37 threes a game, a win SD near 10 and a top scorer near 40. Generated players come in the real league's shares of each position. Fictional leagues are rated on the real league's spread and built with real rosters' heights by roster spot and age mix, so their players develop and age much like real ones (they peak about a year earlier, near 26).
- **Business**: finances that grow with the cap as salaries do, revenue sharing (each team pays or receives 40% of its gap to the league's average local revenue), and owner job security (a fired coach has to take over another team before the league moves on).
- **Coaching carousel** (`src/core/league/coaches.ts`): AI owners review their coaches after the playoffs too, never in season and never the user's team. A coach with two full seasons in charge (one for the league's first coaches, at half the odds) is fired with a chance that rises with how far his team fell short of what its roster should have done over the last season or two (its win% against the league's line through the minutes-weighted OVR of the players who played, each 0.08 a step, the last season counting 60%) and with a record under .400, more for a contender out by the first round, less after a title in the last three seasons, and 0.5-2.5% after a strong season (.600 or the conference finals): about four changes a league season (4.25 over sixteen simulated seasons, real and fictional; the NBA averages about six), seven in ten of them coaches with a losing record. Openings go to the best of the former head coaches out of work (judged on their records shrunk toward .450, titles, Coaches of the Year and playoff runs of the last six seasons; five seasons without a job and they retire from the pool) and two new candidates an opening with new names (no two head coaches in a league's history share one, nor a name the user went by before renaming himself), the better jobs choosing first: about two in five go to former head coaches. A new coach brings his own profile (roots, development, adjustments) and fits his own playbook to the roster, and every firing and hiring makes the news ("The Bulls fired Joe Smith after 4 seasons (140-188).", "The Bulls hired Ann Lee, 2 titles with BOS."). It all draws on a random stream of its own, so the league's own draws are the same with or without it. When the user takes over another team the two coaches swap jobs, each with his own profile (a fired user keeps his job on the books until he does), and Settings won't rename him to a name another coach has had.

**Simplified CBA**

- **Cap and tax**: a soft cap (free agents need cap space unless signed for the minimum), a non-taxpayer mid-level exception (once a season, up to 8.5% of the cap for up to 4 years, only while staying under the tax; afterwards trades can't take the team past the tax line, though minimum signings can), a luxury tax (half of it shared among the teams that didn't pay) and a salary floor. A team that signs a free agent with cap space loses the mid-level for that season and gets a room exception instead (5.7% of the cap, up to 2 years, once); the bi-annual exception (3.3% of the cap, up to 2 years) is for a team that stays under the tax after signing, not two seasons running and not with the room exception, and like the mid-level it keeps trades from taking the team past the tax line. AI teams use them too, the smallest that fits first, and an offer sheet reserves its exception until it's decided (one on cap space holds the space, and the mid-level is gone meanwhile).
- **Contracts**: unlimited re-signing of your own players with cap holds on them until you re-sign or renounce them or free agency opens, player and team options decided after the draft (you choose on your own team options), rookie scale, and dead money for releases (minimum deals signed for the coming season aren't guaranteed until opening night). A release or buyout of a player with seasons left can stretch the dead money evenly over twice the seasons left plus one (in season this season is paid as usual and the rest stretches from next season; in the summer all of it does): the Release confirmation shows both schedules, the cap sheet and Finances label stretched dead money by season, a waiver claim still takes it off the books, the log notes "stretched", and AI teams never stretch. Young players can be qualified instead of re-signed: see Restricted free agency.
- **Extensions**: in the final season of a player's deal you can extend him, any time from the re-sign period through the regular season (not a deal signed this season, a two-way deal, a player with an option still to decide, or one who wants a trade). The extension starts when his current deal ends, runs up to four years (five coming off a rookie-scale deal) with 5% raises and counts on that season's cap like a re-signing. He asks about his market value in that season's money (about 5% less coming off his rookie deal or at 31+, 5% more as a young star, moved by mood and his interest in your team); unhappy players won't talk, and a star on a team he doesn't fancy may want to test free agency, giving the same answer every time you ask that season. An extension shows everywhere the contract does (payroll, the cap sheet and contract tables with EXT, expiring lists, cap holds, dead money, trade value, "Extended through" on the player page), and like a signing it means he can't be traded for a while. AI teams extend about 60% of their willing 76+ players on opening night within their re-signing budgets, and a preseason mail lists your candidates. Extension talks are a negotiation like any other (see Contract talks).
- **Rosters**: 13-15 players in season (up to 21 in the offseason, free agency included; draft picks can take you past 21, and then signings, re-signings included, and player-adding trades wait until you cut back; after the season only next season's contracts count, for two-way slots and two-way conversions too), plus up to three two-way contracts outside the roster and the cap. From January 5 to the end of the regular season a free agent who'd take the minimum can sign a 10-day contract (`src/core/league/tenDay.ts`): his minimum prorated, paid when he signs, ending by itself with no dead money. A team can give a player two a season (a stint with another team in between doesn't reset its count); then it signs him for the rest of the season or he leaves. With four or more players out for 3+ games, the hardship exception allows 10-day players beyond 15 (one for each player out past three). Free Agents has a 10-day mode, the roster, player page and Office show the days left, and the inbox asks on his last day: Second 10-day, Rest of season, or Let him go. AI teams sign 10-days to get back to 13 healthy players.
- **Trades**: 125% + $100K salary matching when over the cap, protected draft picks (yours protected as you trade them), pick swaps, cash (about 5% of the cap each way a season) and the Stepien rule. Players on expiring deals can't be traded after the season, nor can a player whose option is undecided until he decides after the draft. A signing can't be traded until December 15, or three months after an in-season signing; a team can't take back a player or pick it traded away the same season, not even through another team; and a team that used the mid-level can't take its payroll past the tax line in a trade. A contract's surplus or burden in trade value is counted season by season over up to four seasons still to be paid, extension years included, in today's money as the cap grows.
- **Free agency**: free agents can be signed in preseason, the regular season and free agency, and unsigned free agents lower their price every few days, down to what a team that wants them can still pay, until someone bites. A player whose contract runs out, or whose rights you renounce, joins the market like a released one (an asking price, the price-cut clock, and free of his old team's trade-back rule and trade lock). You get the first look when the summer market opens: AI teams start signing on day 2. Top free agents (worth 78+ or asking 8% of the cap or more) weigh offers instead of taking the first one at their price: submit at least 90% of his ask and a day or two later he picks between yours and the AI teams' by the money against what he asks each team, how much he likes each team and the contract length, and the inbox says who he chose and why. Each player has his own priorities (winning, money, playing time, market) weighting how much he likes a team; across the league they average out, so prices hold steady. The offer sheet shows his decision day, who else is bidding, whether your offer leads (and about what would) and how your team fits what matters to him; pending offers hold their cap space, mid-level and roster spot until he decides (one on cap space ends the mid-level while it's pending, as using the space would, just as one on the mid-level keeps the space shut), and you can raise or withdraw them.
- **Contract talks** (`src/core/league/negotiation.ts`): re-signing, free agency, offer sheets and extensions are a short negotiation with the player's agent. His ask is shown, and offering it on his terms signs him at once. He may take a little less, how much he keeps to himself: up to 12% under the ask, less for a team he likes, later in the summer market and for older players, more for money-first players and stars, the same answer every time that season. Below his price he counters between your offer and his ask on the length he wants, and Accept counter takes it. He hears three offers below his price (one more if he's keen on your team, one fewer for a star); a lowball (under 85% of his ask) counts twice and costs some of his interest in your team. When talks break down, in the re-sign period his ask goes up 5% and he takes nothing less; in free agency he won't talk for three days, or the rest of the summer after two lowballs; in season for a week; about an extension for 30 days. Each year off his preferred length costs 3%; a player option on the final season takes 4% off, a team option adds 6%. A player who asked for a trade won't re-sign, nor while a promise only has his request on hold. AI teams sign at the ask, and top free agents still weigh competing offers.
- **Restricted free agency** (`src/core/league/rfa.ts`): in the re-sign period you can extend a qualifying offer to a young player whose deal is up: a first-round pick finishing his rookie-scale deal, or anyone with three seasons or fewer counting the one just played (not a two-way player, nor one coming off a qualifying offer). It is one season at 130% (the #1 pick) to 150% (#30) of a rookie-scale final salary, 125% for anyone else, and at least the minimum plus $0.2M. When the market opens he is a restricted free agent. His team can still re-sign him with Bird rights until he signs an offer sheet, and its cap hold for him (the larger of the offer and his ask) stays on its books through free agency, in trades too, until he signs or the offer is withdrawn. Another team can sign him to an offer sheet: 2-5 years, no options, at what he asks of it, paid for with cap space, the mid-level or the minimum, which stay held until his team decides. His team has two days to match and keep him on exactly those terms (matching needs a roster spot, not cap room), or he joins the other team. Free agency stops when an AI team signs one of your restricted free agents to a sheet; the inbox, the Free Agents screen and the Office offer Match or Let him go, an unanswered sheet lets him go at its deadline, and the new season waits while one is pending. Other teams' restricted free agents show "RFA · BOS can match" and sign offer sheets instead of contracts, and the inbox says whether their team matched and why. A restricted free agent still unsigned when the market closes plays a season on his qualifying offer (if his team has a roster spot) and is unrestricted after it. AI teams qualify the young players worth 66+ they let go (when he's worth at least 80% of the offer on the market), sign restricted free agents worth 68+ to sheets a little more reluctantly than unrestricted ones and one at a time, match when the contract is worth having and fits what they'll pay to keep a young player (their re-signing budget, or up to 5% past the tax), and come to terms with their own after ten days without a sheet. Top free agents who are restricted don't take bids: offer sheets replace them.
- **Waivers and buyouts**: a player released during the regular season with guaranteed money left spends two days on waivers; teams claim in reverse order of record (the AI when the contract is worth having and it has a roster spot and the cap room, or it's a minimum deal), a claim takes over the contract and takes the dead money off the old team's books, and you can claim from the Free Agents screen. Claims close with the regular season, as signings do: anyone still on waivers then clears. Preseason cuts, minimum camp deals and buyouts skip waivers. Veterans (28+) can be bought out from the Release confirmation: they give back up to 35% of what they're owed (more when unhappy, older or on a team that isn't contending), which comes off the dead money. After the trade deadline, rebuilding AI teams with a full roster buy out some veterans on expiring deals, and contenders can pick them up.

**NBA Cup** (`src/core/league/cup.ts`): each season the teams are drawn into six groups of five from pots by last season's record, and each team plays four group games on Cup nights in November (Tuesdays and Fridays, the last one the day after Thanksgiving). The three group winners and the best runner-up in each conference reach the December knockouts: quarterfinals and semifinals at the better seed, then a championship on a neutral floor. Group, quarterfinal and semifinal games count in the standings; the championship counts in neither the standings nor season stats. As in the NBA, 80 games per team are scheduled up front with the knockout window kept clear, and once the groups are decided the teams that didn't advance (and quarterfinal losers) get their last games there, so everyone plays 82. Group ties break on head-to-head, point differential and points scored in group games, then last season's record. The League hub has a Cup tab (group tables, bracket, championship); the winner, runner-up and Cup MVP go into awards and history.

**Trading** (`src/core/league/trades.ts`, `proposals.ts`, `deadline.ts`): the trade dock gives the other GM's two main reasons under the value meter (a veteran worth less to a rebuilder, picks that count extra for a rebuilder or don't help a contender win now, salary that puts them into the tax, players they'd have to waive, depth that doesn't add up to talent, an asset that changed hands this season counting at face value, the two-firsts limit), and warns you when you'd give up far more league-neutral value than you get even if they say yes. When they turn a proposal down, a sheet offers up to three versions they would sign, best for you first: something more from you, one of their pieces left out, or one of their bad contracts for you to absorb. The Find tab lists every player AI teams can trade, filtered by position, OVR, age and the salary you can take back, with each player's value tier (Franchise, All-Star, Starter, Rotation, Depth, or Negative for a contract worth less than nothing) and whether his team looks ready to move him or builds around him; tapping one opens the builder with their ask. AI teams also call with real offers: a team that wants one of your players sends its best package for him (a player who asked out comes up more often), a rebuilder offers a veteran, a taxpayer attaches picks to a contract it wants off its books when free agency opens and you have room, and on draft night teams offer to move up into your pick. Offers come about once every ten days in season and every third day before the deadline, at most four at once and one per team, each standing five to seven days; each is a deal the team accepts and the rules allow, never more than 10% against you in neutral value, accepted from the inbox or the Trade screen's Offers tab (or countered in the builder), and drawn on its own random stream so it never changes a game. Players you mark untouchable are never asked for, in offers or in "what would make this work?". AI-to-AI deals cluster toward the deadline (about thirteen attempts a season, most in its last weeks), with weekly rumors in the last three weeks, a deadline-day briefing and a recap of the season's trades the day after. With "Stop before every pick" the draft waits before each team's pick, so you can trade for the pick on the clock or take offers for yours.

**Deal terms** (`src/ui/trade/Terms.tsx`): a team can protect its own first as it trades it (top 4, 8, 10, 14 or 20: if it lands there it stays home and nothing more is owed; a pick already protected, or another team's, keeps its terms), give another team the right to swap firsts in a future draft (both must hold their own unprotected firsts of that draft; the right rides on the taker's first, goes with it if it's traded, and is settled when the lottery sets the order: if the other first landed higher the two change hands, in the news and the transaction log), and send cash (each team sends and takes up to about 5% of the cap a season, $8.3M in 2026-27; cash isn't salary, comes off the sender's profit and onto the receiver's, outside revenue sharing). AI GMs value a protected pick by the slots it still conveys in, a swap right by its expected gain over both picks' projected slots through their pick lens, and cash at about what second-round picks sell for ($2-3M buys a typical one; rebuilders a little more, contenders less). Labels name the terms everywhere ("2028 1st (NYK) · top-10 prot.", "2029 1st · ATL may swap"), the deal card and dock say what each does ("stays yours about 23% of the time; ATL count it 41% lower"), "What would make this work?" closes a small gap with a little cash before a pick or a player, and a turned-down deal can come back without your protection or with cash added. AI teams never use these terms.

**Transaction log** (`src/core/league/transactions.ts`): every trade (what each side sent), signing and re-signing, extension, release and waiver, claim, buyout, two-way deal and conversion, renounced rights, option decision (yours, and the player opt-outs that make the news), qualifying offer (extended, withdrawn or taken), offer sheet and match, exercised pick swap and draft pick (the first round and yours) is logged with the player's name and the pick labels as they were that day, so it reads the same after he retires or the pick is made. AI teams' quiet roster fills and minimum camp deals aren't listed. Trades and every move of your team stay for good, even after you move to another job; other teams' moves are dropped after two seasons (about 19 KB a season, 55 KB in all). A trade line names swap rights and cash ("for a 2028 1st · top-10 prot., the right to swap 2029 firsts and $1.0M in cash"). Each team page has a Moves tab (by season, the summer as its own block, filtered by trades, signings, releases or other), a player's page has a Transactions card ("Acquired by NYK from BOS with a 2027 1st for …", "Extended by BOS: 4 yrs / $112.0M"), and the Trade screen's partner card lists your last three deals with that team.

AI front offices pick a strategy by where their roster ranks (about ten contenders, ten rebuilders) and blend in their record as the season goes, so a contender at 10-30 starts selling before the deadline. They re-sign within a budget that fits that strategy, qualify their young players and match offer sheets by value and budget, trade among themselves a couple of times a season (both sides have to like the deal, and nobody sends away more than two firsts in one trade), value consolidation and salary dumps honestly, put second-round picks on two-way deals, bring a clearly better free agent into summer camp even with a full roster, and make preseason cuts by value. On casual difficulty their trade discount applies to your own players and picks, not ones you just traded for, so a chain of trades can't compound it. Their strategy lens only adds a premium for what they take in (for a bad contract coming in, at most 15% off its burden): an AI team never sells you its own players or picks below their neutral value, and anything that changed hands this season is valued at face value, so flipping a veteran from a rebuilder to a contender isn't free money. A pick's value moves smoothly toward its draft, and the pick on the clock is worth the prospect it will become. "What would make this work?" adds picks and players together, sends a player when your roster is full, and says whether salary or value was the problem.

stats.nba.com, ESPN and Basketball Reference block many cloud IPs, so everything except the Basketball Reference pages comes from GitHub-hosted mirrors or NBA.com's S3 bucket, which download from anywhere.

EPM exports need a Dunks & Threes subscription, so nothing downloads them: the three regular-season CSVs are committed in `data/epm/` (`scripts/data/epm.ts` reads every `.csv` there, taking the season from its rows, so a new season's export can be dropped in under any name; a season exported twice keeps the later file by name). Players are matched by the NBA.com id the play-by-play crosswalk already has (all 725 of them), else by name and team. Without the folder the build falls back to box plus-minus. The committed seed took EPM with `npm run data:epm` rather than a rebuild: calibration never touches the impact ratings, so each player's moved by what EPM changes in them as derived (Jokić's defensive IQ, carried by his box-score defense, fell 12; Capela's rose 7), the rest of the seed stayed as calibrated, and each real season got its EPM. A full rebuild (`data:build`, or the workflow) takes EPM in on its own.

```bash
npm run data:fetch       # Basketball Reference, ~90 pages (drafts back to 2003) at 1 request / 3.7 s, cached in data/raw/ (-- --refresh re-fetches contracts and current rosters)
npm run data:fetch-nba   # NBA.com play-by-play + matchups (3 seasons) and college box scores, ~90 MB
npm run data:fetch-extra # schedule, pick ownership, injuries, two-way contracts → data/raw/extra/ (-- --force to refresh)
npm run data:build       # parse → profile → RAPM → derive → calibrate in the engine → extras → src/data/seed.json
npm run data:extras      # refresh only the schedule / picks / injuries / two-way part of the current seed
npm run data:epm         # apply data/epm/ to a seed built without it (no recalibration; see below)
```

Or let GitHub do it: run the **Build seed data** workflow (Actions tab → Run workflow). It fetches everything on GitHub's runners, builds and calibrates the seed, verifies the app still builds and passes its tests, commits `src/data/seed.json` to `main` and then runs CI and the Android APK build on it. It also keeps the Basketball Reference pages as a tarball on the `data-raw` branch, so later runs only fetch what's missing, plus the contracts and current rosters, which change all summer and are always fetched again. Its options:

- **commit_seed** (on by default): untick it to only build the seed and upload it as the run's `seed` artifact. A seed that differs from the committed one only in its timestamps isn't committed either way.
- **extras_only**: just refresh the injuries, pick ownership, two-way contracts and schedule in the current seed (no recalibration).
- **refetch**: ignore the pages kept on `data-raw` and fetch all of them again.

If an extra source, or a required Basketball Reference page (contracts, per-game or advanced stats), can't be downloaded, the workflow fails and leaves the committed seed alone. Locally, `data:fetch-extra` only warns: `data:extras` then keeps the seed's current version of that part, and `data:build` falls back to the game's generated version.

The current seed has 544 players: 483 under contract for 2026-27 plus the 61 unsigned players who played 250+ minutes in 2025-26 (the build takes up to 140, most minutes first). 26 players are on two-way deals (23 of them were unsigned in the contracts table, so they join their two-way teams). Six rows of the contracts table are waived players their teams still pay (Ricky Rubio, Didi Louzada, Taj Gibson and three more): the game books that pay as dead money, and those who played in the seed's seasons start as free agents. All 802 NBA.com players from the last three seasons are matched to their Basketball Reference ids. The committed seed was built before the pipeline fetched draft pages from before 2006 or recorded undrafted players' entry classes, so 96 players (LeBron James and 95 undrafted players) arrive without draft data: the game counts their NBA seasons from the stats on record, or from a debut at 22 for veterans who were already playing when the record begins (VanVleet 10), treats undrafted 2026 rookies as rookies, and numbers second-round picks within the round. The pipeline now fetches draft pages back to 2003 and records each undrafted player's entry class, so a rebuilt seed carries the real values.

What's real on opening night, besides the rosters and ratings:

- **Schedule**: the NBA's 2026-27 calendar, from opening night (Oct 20: BOS@DET, PHI@NYK, OKC@SAS) to Apr 11, with the real All-Star break (the trade deadline is the Thursday ten days before the All-Star Game, as in generated seasons). Later seasons, and fictional leagues, use the generated schedule.
- **NBA Cup**: the real groups (East A: BKN, DET, MIL, ORL, TOR …) and their 60 group games on the real Cup nights (Oct 30 to Nov 27), with quarterfinals Dec 4-5, semifinals Dec 8 and the championship Dec 11. The knockout matchups and the two games for each team that doesn't reach them are decided by the simulated group stage, as the NBA will do. If a seed has no Cup data, the missing games go on open December dates instead and there is no Cup that season.
- **Draft picks**: RealGM's ownership rows for 2027-2033 (`scripts/data/extra/picks.ts`). Each pick gets exactly one owner. Pools ("less favorable of MIL and NOP") and swap rights are resolved on a projected draft order (team strength from the seed's ratings, regressed toward the middle for later years), and conditions that depend on other picks ("if DAL conveys its 2027 first", "if not already settled") are checked against picks already resolved. A pick that conveys only outside the top N ("3-30 to Charlotte") is stored as a top-N protected pick. Where several outcomes are possible, the most likely owner gets the pick. The build prints every team's first-rounders and compares them with LD Sport's table.
- **Injuries**: players listed out on opening night start injured. The time out is counted in their team's real games before the expected return date, and a return after the regular season costs playoff games too. ESPN's description is mapped to the game's injury types where one fits (Torn ACL, Hamstring Strain...), otherwise kept descriptive (Torn Labrum). Injuries are off when the league's injury setting is off.
- **Two-way contracts** are marked and don't count toward the 15-man roster.
- **Positions** come from each player's box-score position in his latest season (Basketball Reference's current roster pages only say G / F / C). Rookies without one get theirs from the G/F/C label, size and skills: a guard who handles and passes like a lead guard for his size is a PG, a forward who handles more and rebounds less is an SF.

How ratings are made (`scripts/data/`):

1. **Play-by-play profiles** (`nba/pbp.ts`): one pass over each season builds every player's shot diet by zone (the engine's zones: rim, paint, mid-range, corner three, above-the-break three) with make rates, how he creates (pull-ups, drives, cuts, post moves, putbacks, transition, share of makes assisted), assists by the zone they set up, turnover and foul types, fouls drawn, true rebound rates from on-court opportunities, and rim deterrence (opponents' rim attempts and rim FG% with him on the floor). It also rebuilds every lineup stint.
2. **RAPM** (`nba/rapm.ts`): ridge-regressed adjusted plus-minus over about 200,000 stints from three seasons (recency-weighted, home court modeled), split into offense and defense. Matchup data (`nba/matchups.ts`) adds points saved per 100 possessions guarded, relative to what the same scorers did against everyone else.
3. **Attributes** (`derive.ts`): seasons are weighted by recency and minutes (2025-26 ×1.0, 2024-25 ×0.6, 2023-24 ×0.3), with Bayesian priors on percentages. Finishing, floaters and mid-range come from zone make rates; handling from self-creation and lost-ball rate; passing from assist and bad-pass rates; rebounding from true rebound rates; defense from RAPM, matchup defense, rim deterrence, steals and blocks. Offensive and defensive IQ (and, without play-by-play, perimeter and interior defense) read Dunks & Threes EPM where they used to read box plus-minus, while our RAPM keeps its weight: EPM is box plus-minus done better, a box and tracking prior refined by lineup data. That blend was chosen over leaning on EPM harder (it took over most of RAPM's weight too), splitting RAPM's weight with it, keeping some box plus-minus and adding EPM to perimeter and interior defense, each built and played for 20 `real-check` seasons: the harder lean made simulated team results track the players' real impact worse (r 0.77 against their real EPM, from 0.80), and this one kept them at least as close while moving OVR toward both EPM and RAPM.
4. **Engine-in-the-loop calibration** (`calibrate.ts`): seasons are simulated repeatedly and each player's simulated profile is compared with his real one. Shooting ratings and hidden per-player tendencies are nudged until he reproduces it. The tendencies cover usage, three-point rate, rim, mid-range and corner-three shares, self-creation, passing, turnovers, share of rebounds, steals, blocks and fouls. The tendencies absorb role and opportunity, so they don't distort the visible ratings. To keep them from overfitting one context, half the calibration games use next season's rosters and half the 2025-26 teams the stats came from, each game draws the coach's playbook from his top three, and players randomly miss games so rotations get re-planned around them.
5. **Rookies and small samples**: 2026 draftees are shaped by their college production (shooter, passer, rim protector...) and leveled to their draft slot and reputation. Short NBA samples are shrunk toward the level their minutes suggest; calibration then settles again.
6. **OVR** is a fitted, size-aware blend of attributes. The weights were fitted so that real players' ratings predict half regressed box plus-minus and half RAPM, plus the minutes and usage coaches gave them (with EPM in the impact ratings, OVR vs EPM r ≈ 0.92, vs RAPM r ≈ 0.78, vs BPM r ≈ 0.85; 0.91, 0.76 and 0.88 before).

`npm run real-check` simulates a season with the real league and compares every regular with his real 2025-26 numbers. With the current seed, sim-vs-real correlations over about 227 regulars are 0.91 for points, 0.90 for assists, 0.93 for rebounds, 0.92 for blocks, 0.88 for threes made, 0.83 for steals, 0.82 for minutes per game and 0.68 for true shooting, and each team's net rating follows its players' real EPM at r 0.85 and their real box plus-minus at r 0.88, from 0.83 and 0.87 without EPM in the ratings (averages over 20 seeds). Minutes follow because coaches remember last season's roles, counted once and converted to full-strength minutes, so real rotations carry over without burying healthy veterans. Every league per-game rate (pace, points, threes, free throws, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, turnovers, fouls) lands within 5% of the 2025-26 NBA, and shot diets match too: about 0.93 for rim share, 0.88 for mid-range share and 0.93 for three-point share. It also prints the game shape (margin spread, blowouts, overtime, win spread), each team's net rating against its players' real box plus-minus and EPM, the leaders, single-game highs and the DPOY ladder, so outliers are easy to spot.

The pipeline is covered by parser and pipeline unit tests (CSV, RAPM, name matching, EPM), an offline end-to-end fixture and seed sanity tests. The fixture writes Basketball Reference-style pages (and EPM exports) for a simulated fictional league, so the build (parse, derive, calibrate) runs offline in about a minute and a half:

```bash
npx tsx scripts/data/test/make-fixture.ts /tmp/fx && HC_RAW_DIR=/tmp/fx HC_SEED_OUT=/tmp/fx-seed.json npx tsx scripts/data/build-seed.ts
```

The data scripts read a few environment variables: `HC_RAW_DIR` (where the raw downloads live, default `data/raw`; the NBA.com and extras folders inside it follow unless `HC_NBA_DIR` or `HC_EXTRA_DIR` point elsewhere), `HC_EPM_DIR` (the EPM exports, default `data/epm`, or `epm` inside `HC_RAW_DIR` when that is set), `HC_SEED_OUT` (the seed that `data:build`, `data:extras` and `data:epm` write, default `src/data/seed.json`), `HC_REPORT` (`data:build` also writes a per-player JSON report there: minutes, advanced stats, RAPM, EPM, ratings and tendencies) and `HC_PICK_DEBUG` (a pick such as `2027:1:DAL`, whose competing ownership claims the picks resolver prints).

## Ship to phones

- **PWA**: deploy `dist/` to any static host and use "Add to Home Screen". It works offline after the first load, and saves stay on the device (IndexedDB, with persistent storage requested). Saves are incremental (game results live in their own store and only new ones are written; older ratings snapshots keep just OVR and potential, so a long career grows by about 0.4 MB a season), a live-game journal that's damaged or isn't for the game still to be played today is dropped on load, a failed save says so, offers a backup and keeps retrying (a brand-new league included), opening another league, importing a backup or starting a new one saves the open league first (and offers an export when it can't), and a league open in two windows can't overwrite itself. A new version downloads in the background and applies on the next launch, never mid-game; reloading after an update or from the error screen saves the league first.
- **Backups**: Settings → Export backup saves the league as a JSON file (the share sheet in the app, a download on the web). Import it from Settings or the start screen on any device.
- **Android**: the _Android APK_ workflow builds a debug APK on every push to `main` (download it from the run's artifacts). Pushing a `v*` tag attaches the APK to a release. Locally: `npm run cap:sync && npx cap open android`. The native shell adds the hardware Back button (closes the topmost sheet first, goes Home when there is no earlier screen, saves before leaving), haptics, edge-to-edge insets and no service worker (the files ship in the APK).
- **Android signing**: Android only installs an update signed with the same key as the installed app. Without signing secrets, each workflow run signs with a throwaway debug key, so a new build only installs after uninstalling the old one, which deletes its saves (export a backup first). To sign every build with one key, create a keystore once and keep it safe (a lost key means users must reinstall):
  ```bash
  keytool -genkeypair -v -keystore release.keystore -alias hoops -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000
  base64 -w0 release.keystore   # paste the output into ANDROID_KEYSTORE_BASE64
  ```
  Then add four repository secrets (Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions): `ANDROID_KEYSTORE_BASE64`, `ANDROID_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD`, `ANDROID_KEY_ALIAS` (`hoops` above) and `ANDROID_KEY_PASSWORD`. Debug APKs are then signed with that key, and `v*` tags also get a signed release APK (`app-release.apk`) on the release. The version code is the workflow's run number, so each build counts as newer than the last.
- **iOS**: `npm run cap:sync && npx cap open ios` on a Mac with Xcode.
- **GitHub Pages**: run the _Deploy PWA to Pages_ workflow manually after setting Settings → Pages → Source to "GitHub Actions". It builds for the site's own path (`/<repo>/` for a project site, `/` for a user site or a custom domain). Pages on a private repo needs a paid GitHub plan.

## Design decisions

- Ratings are shown on a 2K-style 40-99 scale, fully visible. OVR is anchored to the league's distribution, so 93+ is an MVP candidate, 87+ an All-Star, 82+ a quality starter and about 74 the median rostered player. Attributes use the same 40-99 scale (league median ≈ 68).
- The contracts table includes camp deals, so some teams start with 16-20 players. AI teams make preseason cuts down to 15 standard contracts at league creation (two-way contracts don't count). If your team is over, the Assistant GM suggests who to release before opening night.
- Free agents and AI trades are tuned for casual play by default (difficulty can be changed in Settings).
- Future picks start with their real owners (2027-2033). The game's pick model is one owner per pick plus an optional top-N protection and swap right, so RealGM's conditions are simplified (seed swaps are still resolved on the projected order): swaps and "more/less favorable of" pools go to the projected outcome, protections that send the pick to a third team, or roll over to a later year, are approximated as "stays with the original team that year, obligation ends", and picks the NBA has forfeited stay with their original team.
- The real schedule is used for 2026-27 only. Games the NBA hasn't published yet are filled on open December dates.
- Team logos are original monograms in each franchise's colors (no trademarked artwork).
