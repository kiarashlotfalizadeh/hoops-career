# Hoops Career

A mobile-first basketball GM **and** head-coach game, built as an installable offline PWA with Capacitor shells for Android and iOS. It starts in the 2026-27 season with real rosters (New League also offers a fictional league with the real franchises), and its main feature is a tactics layer that tells you _why_ things happen.

## What's in the box

**Possession-by-possession engine** (`src/core/sim/engine.ts`, its parts in `src/core/sim/engine/`: one module per part of a game, from the pick-and-roll to rebounds, fouls and substitutions)

- Every half-court trip runs a concrete action: pick-and-roll, isolation, post-up, handoffs/off-ball screens, or motion/cuts/drive-and-kick. Transition, putbacks, presses and end-of-game situations are modeled too.
- Defense chooses its pick-and-roll coverage (drop, switch, hedge, blitz, ice), base scheme (man, 2-3, 3-2), help level, ball pressure, physicality, transition defense, press and a star plan (deny or double). The 3-2 zone contests threes harder than man-to-man but is softer in the paint, post, cuts and glass, so it beats the 2-3 against shooting teams and loses to post-up and drive teams; switching off the ball denies handoffs, but switched handoffs and cross-screens hand out mismatch isolations and post-ups where the smaller defender fouls. A box-and-one (four in a 2-2 zone, one chaser) or triangle-and-two (three in a zone, two chasers) face-guards their main creators: fewer touches, late catches, a defense that loads up on him sooner, few catch-and-shoot looks and a tiring chase, while a defender caught high gives up drives on the catch and back-cuts, and everyone else gets more open threes, gaps and offensive boards. Named for players in a game plan or live, it plays man-to-man while they sit. Against a star with non-shooters around him a named box-and-one is worth about +1.5 points per 100 possessions; against a shooting team about −4.6.
- Each tactical effect is tagged with its expected-points value (e.g. "drop coverage conceded pull-ups: +3.4"). That is what drives the post-game report and debrief, the live bench's calls and what it says each of your moves did.
- Rotations follow a minute-by-minute stint plan built from your depth chart. The staff overrides it for fouls, fatigue, injuries and blowouts (hard minute caps hold: a capped player sits down up to 40 seconds early, since subs wait for a dead ball), and playoff rotations shorten to about eight (the starters and closers always among them). The starters open both halves, and the closers finish the game; the coach's starting and closing fives take the floor whatever their positions. A fouled-out or injured player is replaced by whoever the staff wants on the floor at that point, so he isn't subbed straight back out. A reserve who starts for an injured or resting starter takes over that starter's minutes. Garbage time empties the bench in real blowouts only: the starters come straight back if the game gets within reach, and nobody subs in a whole new five in the last half minute. You can also sub manually during live games.
- NBA rules at the edges of the game: teams get a shot off before the buzzer (a team tied or down three or less gets one off with a second or two left; a team protecting a late 4th-quarter lead runs out the clock instead), the shot clock runs from the inbound (breaking a press uses some of it) and a side-out foul restarts it at 14 or more, a timeout stops play (the trip out of it is an inbound, never a run-out) and in a live ball only the team with the ball can call one, hack-a-player stops in the last two minutes of every period, trailing teams foul only while a foul can still buy another possession (never down three; down four to six from the last 30 seconds), a team down three late hunts a three and one down two plays for the tie, at most two timeouts after the 3:00 mark of the 4th, and short-handed teams never play four (a player with six fouls stays in when nobody is left, and each foul from his sixth on adds a technical; an injured player with no healthy sub is replaced by the last player disqualified). Box scores add up to exactly 48 minutes a team (plus 5 per overtime), and the starters are the five who took the floor for the tip.
- AI coaches adjust during games: at quarter breaks and timeouts, how often they change coverage, help, pressure or the glass depends on their adjustments rating (one beaten by a two-man game or a pick-and-pop they can switch takes it away by switching ball screens). Your assistant does the same in your simmed games while the staff runs your tactics, and when you run them, if you ask it to (Tactics → Match); without it the other bench kept cashing in on what it found, a switch hunted all night with the smaller man fouling. Game reports list both benches' adjustments by quarter.
- The end of a close game: a team holding for the last shot starts its play with eight to ten seconds left when tied (sooner when behind) and shoots with a second or few to spare, so a miss can still be put back; a trip begun with the game nearly over gets a scrambled look. About two games in a hundred are won at the horn (the NBA's about one), and the rest of the late winners come through the last 24 seconds.
- Calibrated to 2025-26 league averages: pace ≈ 100, ORtg ≈ 114, ~41% of shots from three, about 27 assists per team game with NBA-like assisted shares, and turnover, rebounding, foul and block rates within 5%. Games vary like the NBA's (a margin SD near 13 for a given matchup, about 16% decided by 20+, about 4% to overtime, the two scores moving together), and team quality spreads the standings: team net ratings track what the players' real box plus-minus (BPM) predicts at the minutes they get (r ≈ 0.86), and win SD is about 11 (the NBA's is about 12). Offensive fouls are about 1.8 a team game (0.3 of dead-ball turnovers), the inbound after a side-out foul takes 2 to 4 seconds, and a defense protecting a 1-3 point lead in the last 24 seconds plays without reaching in. Ball pressure and the blitz force turnovers about as much as the NBA's most aggressive defenses do, a trap's mostly through the trappers' hands: the league's forced turnovers spread like the NBA's (the best ball-hawking defense about 1.1 to 1.3 times the average, not 1.5), and trapping pays only the quickest hands. Tactic extremes have real costs, so no slider setting is a free win. Tactics matter: every matchup edge (a coverage against the action it faces, a zone against a shooting or a post team, a junk defense, a set against the defense it beats, a guarding instruction, a star plan, pressure against loose or secure handlers) is played at a tactical leverage of 2 (`K.tacticEdge`), so the gap between the right call and the wrong one is about twice what it was (right calls about +0.8 points per 100 on average, wrong ones about −1.2), while a setting chosen blind is worth no more than about 1.5 and the league's averages stay where they were. `scripts/tactic-impact.ts` measures each aimed choice with paired seeds. A full season sims in about 5 to 6 s on a laptop (a game is about 1.35 ms in the engine), in a Web Worker (`src/workers/sim.worker.ts`), so the screens stay responsive and show the league as it stands every 20 days.

**Coaching & understanding**

- **Tactics** (Team → Tactics, laid out as Football Manager 26's tactics screen: few big decisions on the surface, every setting one tap down). The hero holds Tactic A/B/C, the style (offensive playbook and defensive identity) with Change, one familiarity figure, the system's two **strengths** and two **weaknesses** (they move as you edit) and the assistant's switch; then four sections. **Tactic** is the **tactics board**: your five on a half court where the shape puts them in possession and the scheme out of possession, each with his role at that end and how well he suits it (★), the **partnerships** between them drawn as lines (heavier the more the engine plays them; a red line where two bigs crowd the paint), a Partnerships card (each link, its standing and the pair's understanding, the clash and its one-tap fix, the bench's best pair), the bench as a list and My playbooks as one row; tapping a player opens his sheet: both roles, each offensive role with the partnerships it would make with the five, and his instructions (shot freedom, glass duty, preferred ball screener). **Instructions** has the mentality and FM26's three phases of play, each with its familiarity: **In possession** (Play through: Balanced, Pick-and-roll, Handoffs, Motion, Inside or Isolation, one choice that sets the five action weights; the shot profile; the tempo), **Transition** (after a stop; when a shot goes up, one dial from Crash hard to Sprint back over the offensive glass and getting back; the press) and **Out of possession** (scheme, ball-screen coverage, help), each phase's other settings in its Fine-tune, and the set package under In possession. Every option shows how well the team knows it and every control moved off where it's drilled what that costs. **Match** has crunch time and situations, the late-game rules and timeouts in More rules. **Analysis** is the system report, familiarity (by phase, the practice focus and the players) and the season's evidence. Analysis carries the **system report** (`src/core/analysis/system.ts`): seven things your system should be good at on each end (shot creation, spacing, rim pressure, ball movement, transition, second chances, ball security; rim protection, perimeter D, pick-and-roll D, three-point D, the defensive glass, transition D, forcing turnovers), each graded A+ to F by where it ranks against the other 29 teams' systems (their rotations and tactics), and each a tap from what it's made of: the players who carry it (minutes-weighted, the best one counted extra where one man carries it) and what every setting adds or takes away, in the directions the engine plays them (help packs the paint and leaves shooters, a 3-2 zone guards the arc and gives up the glass, crashing the glass leaks run-outs, a drop needs a rim protector, a switch needs defenders of a size, pressure forces turnovers). The hero's strengths and weaknesses move as a setting does, and the changes dock says what the visit's edits did to the grades ("Second chances D → A+", "Transition D A → C"), so every lever shows what it buys and what it costs. Each player's sheet shows every role at both ends with its stars (`roleFits`, `defRoleFits`) and how well he'd know that pair; Analysis adds what the season's games say is working and hurting (in points a game, from the engine's tags) and how other teams read you. Every 0-100 setting is picked from five named levels (pace: Grind it out, Patient, Balanced, Up-tempo, Run & gun; each action from Almost never to Heavily) and explained in place, each explanation behind a "?". A fit is a starting score plus what each roster quality adds or takes away (lead creator, shooting, a passing big, rim protection, depth...), and each list sorts best fit first with the fit as a figure and a bar. Each playbook's fit is fitted to what it measured in paired engine games for rosters like yours (a fit point is about 0.1 points per 100; a coach's own philosophy adds 3, enough to break near-ties), so the best fit is the one the games reward, and the assistant picks by it: letting the assistant manage beats the old fit-by-feel picks by about 2.3 points per 100 over 210 rosters. When the staff runs your tactics it also acts on the season's own evidence: a setting whose trade-off the games say costs more than it wins back is changed (and stays changed until its cost has halved), and the lead assistant mails what he changed and what it was costing; for tactics you run yourself the same evidence is a staff card with its one-tap change. The staff's rotation plays about ten and a half players, no stint under six minutes, and a starter it would play under 22 minutes swaps with the reserve who'd play eight more. Tapping a playbook opens its sheet: what it is, its fit and rank, the starting score and what each roster quality adds or takes away (they add up to the fit), the players behind each, what would raise it, and Use. Each phase of Instructions opens on the style it starts from ("3 settings moved from it", with Put back), and a dot marks the style's own setting on every control. Presets, the staff's in-game adjustments and situational plans can land between the levels: the level nearest the value is the one shown, and the engine plays the value itself.
- **The system, Football Manager-style** (`src/core/sim/familiarity.ts`, `src/core/sim/slots.ts`, `src/core/league/practice.ts`): a coach keeps three tactics, **A, B and C** (the active one is the team's tactics; the others are drilled in practice too, a little less), switches between them on the Tactics screen or in a live game, and a situational plan can switch to one while its situation lasts. Each tactic has a **shape**, its half-court formation: 4-Out 1-In (the league's default and the engine's baseline), 5-Out (an open paint for drives, rolls and cuts, worth less for every non-shooter on the floor, and fewer offensive boards; a big who can't shoot makes it a loss), Horns (handoffs as good as the better elbow big's passing, fewer turnovers, less motion) and the Double Post (its two bigs finish better inside and win more boards as far as they rebound; everyone else's drives find a crowded paint, more so when the bigs are shooters wasted under the basket). Over the real league's rosters each is worth about −0.1, +0.2 and +0.3 points per 100 on average against four-out, from about −2.4 to +1.8 by roster; the staff plays another shape only when it fits by 0.4 points per 100 or more, so about a fifth of the league leaves four-out. Every player has an **in-possession role** (the ten offensive roles) and an **out-of-possession role**: Position Defender, Point-of-Attack Stopper (picks up their lead handler: fewer blow-bys and more turnovers on the ball, a tiring chase), Wing Stopper (their best scorer who isn't the handler: fewer touches, tougher shots), Rim Anchor (their least dangerous big, contesting everyone's shots at the rim; a stretch big he leaves gets open looks), Roamer (off their weakest shooter into the driving lanes; his man's kick-outs come open) or Hidden (out of the action); the matchups send each role to its man, and each player's suitability for every role at both ends is shown in stars. A player can also be told to crash the offensive glass or get back on every shot. The **mentality** (very cautious to very aggressive) nudges pace, pushing, crashing, the shot diet, ball pressure, physicality and getting back together; away from balanced it costs about 0.3 to 1 point per 100 in an even game (each step about 2.5 possessions a game) and buys possessions and variance, so it's for protecting or chasing a game.
- **Familiarity**: a team knows each option it could play (a scheme, a coverage, a shape, a press) to a degree that games and practice build and that fades, when it's not played, back to what any NBA team knows of it (man-to-man and the drop are known by everyone, a triangle-and-two by nobody); its sliders are drilled at the values it plays (moving one costs familiarity as far as it moves); every player learns the system (a new arrival starts at 35%) and the pair of roles he plays. The screens show it in seven parts (shape, actions & shots, tempo, roles; scheme, coverage, pressure & help) from New to Fluid. Below fluid the engine plays a unit a step slow: worse looks and more turnovers on offense, more open shots given up on defense, tagged in the reports (`scripts/system-impact.ts`, against the same tactics drilled: a never-drilled coverage costs about 0.4 points per 100, a never-drilled 2-3 zone about 0.6, a new shape about 0.25, a new offense with new roles for everyone about 1.8, and about ten games of it close most of the gap). Training camp before opening night drills what each team opens with (a new system lands near 80%). Between games a team practices at its **focus**: Balanced, Tactical drills (familiarity and new players' knowledge about twice as fast), Opponent prep (whatever the game plan changes for the next opponent is drilled for that game, while the system builds slowly) or Recovery (wear comes off about twice as fast, nothing builds); practice intensity scales it. AI coaches keep their system in season unless another fits clearly better (a new coach installs his own, and pays for it), and an AI coach who comes in with a game plan against you has drilled it.
- **Partnerships** (`src/core/sim/links.ts`): two players whose roles complement each other on the floor form a link the engine plays in its own action, with no control of its own: a handler (Primary Creator, Secondary Scorer or Playmaker) and a Rim Runner run a **two-man game** (more of his screens are the big's, the handler's edge and the roll finish are better), a handler and a Stretch Big a **pick-and-pop**, a Post Hub and a Movement Shooter or Glue Guy play **inside-out** (cleaner kick-outs), a Playmaking Hub and a Movement Shooter or Slasher a **handoff chain** (handoffs and cuts), and on defense a Point-of-Attack Stopper with a Rim Anchor behind him is **the wall** (the handler he guards has less of an edge). A link plays at its fit (what the two bring to it, a geometric mean: it's only as good as both) times the pair's **understanding**, built by minutes on the floor together (24 minutes together close 12% of the way to 100, about 15 games of them take a new pair to gelled; practice adds a little; a pair a trade just put together starts at 35 and plays at none of it, a pair at 90 at all of it; training camp leaves the rotation's pairs at 90). Two inside bigs (Rim Runners, Post Hubs) clash instead: they **crowd the paint** for everyone else's drives and cuts, except in the Double Post, the shape built for two bigs (the staff counts that in its shape choice). Measured with paired games (`scripts/link-impact.ts`, each team's pairs gelled against new to each other): about +0.5 points per 100 for a real roster's links on average (−0.1 to +1.0 by team), +0.7 for an elite two-man game, +1.4 for an elite pick-and-pop, −0.14 for a crowded starting five; with every pair gelled the league scores about 0.8 per 100 more than with every pair new. The board draws them, the role picker shows what each role would make with the five, the staff suggests a role that makes one (or ends a crowd), the matchup preview names each five's, the trade dock says which a deal breaks, and the reports tag them (each tag weighted to what it measures).
- **Situational plans and crunch time**: up to four situational plans (Team → Tactics → Match) change your tactics while a situation lasts (a quarter range, time left, the score, or the second unit on the floor): the first enabled plan that matches plays on top of your base tactics until the situation ends, and the play-by-play says so. Live edits always go to the base tactics. A go-to player gets the ball much more in the last two minutes of a close fourth quarter or overtime (with none named, the best scorer on the floor does), and an after-timeout play opens your next half-court trip after your timeouts. Game plans can name hack-a-player targets and the quarter hacking starts, and a player hacked twice in a period goes to the bench when a reserve his size can come in. Foul to give (Tactics → Match) fouls the ball handler at once when the other team has the ball with about 3 to 8 seconds left in a period and a foul is only a side out (in the 4th and overtime only when tied or up by three or less), so they inbound with less time and a worse first look; about one in twenty is called on the shot. AI teams use none of these, so the league's numbers are unchanged.
- **Sets** (`src/core/sim/sets.ts`): your offense can run seven named sets: Spain pick-and-roll, Horns, Floppy, Elevator doors, Hammer, Chicago and Post split. Each is run for the player on the floor who fits it best (or one you name) and goes for its own look: the roll, an elbow touch or a pop, a curl or a catch-and-shoot three, the corner kick, the split cut. Nothing in a set makes anyone shoot better: each has edges against the defenses it's built to beat (Spain against drop and hedge, Floppy and Chicago against fighting over screens, Hammer against heavy help) and none, or worse, against the ones that kill it (switching, zones, staying home), and takes a second or two more of the shot clock. Defenses read sets: each run in a game makes its look a little less open and its passes easier to jump, sooner against coaches rated high at tactics, so a set is strongest early (a first run is worth about +0.1 to +0.3 points per possession against the defense it beats; each read costs about 0.1 to 0.25). Team → Tactics → Offense keeps a package of up to four that your half court mixes in on fresh trips (never in late-game situations, only for players who fit, and your staff stops running one once the defense is on it) and any set can be your after-timeout play, run for a player you name. A package is a small edge, not a free one: about +0.45 points per 100 possessions at best against a drop, about −0.2 against switching (paired engine games, `scripts/tactic-impact.ts`). Game and period reports list each set's runs, points per possession and the run that got it read. AI coaches run no sets, so their games play exactly as before.
- **Saved playbooks** (`src/core/sim/playbooks.ts`): "My playbooks" keeps up to twelve named playbooks of your own (schemes, every slider, your set package, game management, and roles, shot freedom and screeners by player, which only load for players still on your roster; matchups, star, chase and hunt targets and instructions by player are game by game and aren't saved). Each shows what loading it would change ("Pace 62 → 80", "Pick-and-roll coverage Drop → Hedge") and can be loaded, overwritten, renamed or deleted; the header names the playbook your tactics came from, with "· edited" once they move from it. The library is the coach's, so it comes along when you take over another team.
- **Game plans** (`src/core/league/gamePlans.ts`, the Plan tab of each opponent's matchup page, `/games/:tid`): a plan for each opponent, for the next game against them, the current playoff series or the rest of the season. Its items replace one another by kind (one ball-screen coverage, one star plan...) and change actions relative to your playbook, so they build on whatever you run; matchups can be planned too. The Plan tab sets the scope, lists the items with the evidence behind them and the assistant's counters to add (Add for this opponent, Always for every game), sets a star plan on any of their top eight, and the Matchups tab plans matchups: their projected top eight against your top ten, with the staff's tip-off assignment (from the engine's own matchup solver, `src/core/sim/matchups.ts`) and what each pairing means (size gap, his creation against your defender's perimeter D). A plan can also set a junk defense and who it chases, how to guard each of their top eight (`src/core/analysis/guarding.ts`: go under screens, top-lock him off the line, sag off him, get physical; each changes only the actions he's in, with the staff's ★ suggestion from his games or his ratings), and which of their defenders to attack (your isolations, post-ups and ball screens go at the man he guards, which costs some ball movement; a coach being hunted with success hides him on your weakest scorer). The Matchups tab and the live player sheet set the same instructions, and none of it is kept beyond that opponent. The assistant suggests a box-and-one or triangle-and-two against a one- or two-man offense whose others can't shoot, and attacking a real weak link, with the numbers.
- **Scouting from the games played** (`src/core/analysis/scouting.ts`): each opponent's matchup page (its Scout tab) reads its actual games: pace, offensive and defensive ratings, the four factors at both ends, three-point and rim rates with league ranks, play types at both ends with points per possession against the league's (and how unusual each is), key players with on/off numbers and your meetings with them, read into plain-English tendencies and ways to attack them (last season's numbers, labeled, until a team has played five games, without play types: of last season's results the save keeps only your games). The assistant's counters are drafted against the plan you already have for that opponent, and those the numbers back carry that evidence and come first (sprint back against a transition team, defend without fouling against a team that lives at the line, press after makes against loose handlers, post-ups against a weak post defense...).
- **Opposing game plans** (`src/core/ai/opponentPlan.ts`): AI coaches scout you too. On normal a coach comes in with a plan for about half your games (more often the better he is at tactics), on hard every coach does, and on casual none do. A plan is up to two changes to his usual tactics, each answering something your games show (a hedge or ice against a pick-and-roll you score well from, a drop against hot rollers when he has a rim protector, staying home on a three-point team or packing the paint against one that can't shoot, sprinting back, defending without fouling, ball pressure on loose handlers, running against a team that doesn't get back, denying or doubling the player you run your offense through, or a box-and-one on him when the rest of your team doesn't make its threes) or exposes something in yours (attacking your weakest perimeter defender when he gives up a lot to the rest of your rotation, or guarding one of your top seven the way your own staff would read him: going under on a handler who can't pull up, top-locking a shooter who can't drive, sagging off a non-shooter, getting physical with a poor free-throw shooter inside). The matchup page says "Expect from them tonight: ices ball screens, denies Brunson the ball" (with the numbers behind each item), the preview plays their plan exactly as the game will, the live period report lists it, and the report's Why tab keeps "Their game plan". Whether he plans, and what, comes from the game's id and your games before game day, so it never touches the league's random stream and AI-vs-AI games are unchanged.
- **Pre-game** (the matchup page's Preview tab, `src/core/analysis/preview.ts`): Watch & coach (the Continue bar on game day) opens tonight's matchup page on its Preview first, with Tip off and Quick sim in its dock (Settings → Pre-game screen turns this off; Watch & coach live in the Play options always goes straight to the game). It opens on the staff's briefing: the lead assistant's read of them (who to watch and why), what they run on offense and defense and where to hit them (the scouting report), and his plan for tonight (the counters the numbers back first, one per kind), each a tap into your game plan, or Use the staff's plan for all of them. The plan is held to what measures well in paired games (following it is worth about +1 point per 100 and +3 points of win%): he never suggests a zone (it stays on the Defense tab), doubles only a star who can't pass out of it with non-shooters around him, and offers a box-and-one or triangle-and-two only against a cast that can't shoot by its ratings, not just a cold month. AI coaches plan against you the same way. Then both teams' records, conference ranks, form and ratings with league ranks, back-to-backs and playoff stakes ("R1 · Game 5", elimination games); a projection (this season's net ratings, weighted toward what each rotation is worth tonight until enough games are in, plus about 1.8 points of home court, as the engine plays it: home teams win about 54.7%); both injury reports (out, resting, worn down) and both projected starting fives as the game will start them; the opponent's play types against the league's, its defense and how often its coach adjusts; and their coach's plan for tonight; who guards whom and your game plan are the next tabs over. Storylines (`src/core/analysis/storylines.ts`) set up the night: a player facing a team he played for ("for the first time since he left" only when every game since is stored), a career milestone one good night away, team streaks and a player's run of 20-point nights, last season's playoff rematch, the MVP race and two No. 1 picks. The injury report makes the game-time decisions: a player one game from back from a minor or moderate injury (not a concussion) is questionable and sits unless you clear him, and cleared he plays about 6% below his best and twice as likely to get hurt in the game, with the game counting as the one he had left (the starters, the projection and a resumed live game all play it that way; other teams always sit theirs). Today's next-game card says when it is and lists who is out on both sides, the series, whether a plan is set and the lead assistant's one line on them (who to watch, where to hit them), and the Continue bar names the opponent ("vs BOS (12-8) · today"). Keys to the game (`src/core/analysis/keys.ts`) give you three numbers to hit tonight, read from both teams' games before today: what they do best to take away (keep them off the offensive glass, run them off the three-point line, defend without fouling, get back in transition, protect the paint), what their defense does to teams (take care of the ball against one that forces turnovers; crash the glass, get to the line or let it fly against one that gives those up), making the scorer they run it through work, and sharing the ball. They're picked strongest signal first (standard deviations from the league's teams, never two on one number) and each target is about where tonight's number lands if both teams play to form (their own and what the other allows, halved), so a little under half are met (the offensive glass is read as a share of the misses, since a count grows with a team's own misses); over three teams' seasons, the games with none met were won 31% of the time, one 40%, two 63% and all three 78%. In a later season's first five games they read last season's team totals (its results keep only your games); on a new league's opening nights, with no games to read, they come from the other coach's tactics and his players' last season. Because they read only the games before tonight, the preview, the live game and the recorded result all have the same three.
- **Live game**: an animated 2D court driven by the engine's possession logs, with a bench under it that says what's happening and lets you act on it. Its touchline (`src/ui/live/Touchline.tsx`) switches between Tactic A, B and C in one tap (tonight's game plan and your orders on their players carry over), sets the mentality and the shape, and every scheme, coverage, press and shape there shows how well the team knows it, so a switch to something unpracticed is visibly a gamble; the night's moves and the report name each switch. The court is the home team's floor (its colours in the paint and in an apron round the court, its mark at center court and its name behind the basket; a neutral floor for the Cup final), and the scorebug gives each team a block in its colours (the away side wears its second colour when the two would read as one). It's laid out like a broadcast: a slim scorebug over a full-width court, the console under it, and the bench bar in the tab bar's place until the final (Timeout, with the ones left, then the console's views: Bench, Game plan, Lineup and Stats; the scorebug carries the counts: Timeouts, Fouls, Penalty in the bonus, and To win). "More bench" at the top of the console makes the court smaller on a phone for more of it; held sideways, the court takes the left side and the scorebug, the bar and the console the right. The **Bench** (`src/ui/live/staff.ts`) is the lead assistant's (a name of his own on each team you run, `leadAssistant`): his read of the game in a sentence (the biggest edges each way in the box score), then the calls he wants from you, each with its evidence and the moves on offer, his pick first: a run the staff won't stop (Timeout), tired legs and foul trouble when you make the subs or in crunch time (the sub), their hot scorer and who's been on him (your best perimeter defender on him, deny him or double him), a play type of theirs running hot (its counter: "drop is giving their handlers room", Hedge), the biggest tactical edge against you (its one-tap fix), the defender of theirs you're scoring on (Attack him), your own actions stalling or working (lean away from them or into them), and the late-game fouls when they're off and matter. Who's scoring on whom comes from the possession logs: each trip is credited to the player who finished it and the defender on him. - **Business**: finances that grow with the cap as salaries do, crowds that fill each franchise's own arena (17,000 to 21,000 seats, `src/core/teams.ts`) as hype, winning, market size and the ticket price draw them, a hyped visitor drawing more (AI front offices price where the gate peaks: a hot ticket sells out, a losing team with no buzz draws about 15,000), revenue sharing (each team pays or receives 40% of its gap to the league's average local revenue), and an owner who judges you against your roster (`src/core/league/ownerGoals.ts`, Office → Owner). On opening night, after the AI's cuts and signings, he sets three or four goals from the roster you open with, frozen for the season (`League.ownerGoals`): a win total (what its minutes-weighted OVR, played as an assistant would play it with each injured player back when he's due, should win against the league's average roster on the projection's net-rating line), a postseason goal from where that projects you in your conference and your front office's strategy (a contender or a top-four roster a playoff round, the conference finals when it's the conference's best by four projected wins and the Finals by nine; fifth to eighth the playoffs, ninth and tenth the play-in), for a rebuild or a roster projected 11th or worse a development goal instead (minutes for players 23 or under: what an assistant's rotation gives them, plus what young players earn as they improve; a retooling team that plays its young players gets one too), and a money goal: the season's profit on his budget (revenue and costs at today's ticket price, hype, market and payroll, revenue sharing, the salary-floor shortfall, a typical season's playoff gates and tax payouts, and the gate a winning roster adds or a losing one loses) or, with the payroll from 6% under the luxury tax to just over it (4% over for a frugal owner), staying or getting under it, and for a taxpayer beyond that keeping the payroll where it is. Each franchise's owner is a builder, win-now or frugal (a hash of the league and the team, never its random stream), which moves the targets (a win or two, a few million) and the weights a little, and a small market weighs money more. Over sixteen simulated league-seasons (two real and two fictional leagues, 480 team-seasons) the win targets were unbiased (actual wins 0.95 × his expectation + 2.0, r 0.72, a miss's SD 7 wins; within 1.7 wins on average in every band of expectation and for every front-office strategy that night), and every kind of goal grades near zero on average (wins +1, postseason +7, profit 0, payroll +3, development +1, the season +1 with an SD of 33). Office → Owner shows a job-security meter and each goal's progress (ahead, on track, behind, met or missed; the wins projected as the season forecast projects them, so it agrees with the odds line; in the preseason, the goals he'd set with today's roster), his personality, the standing and the past reviews; Today shows the meter when the seat is warm, and he writes at the All-Star break with every goal and once if the forecast has you five wins under his target after 40 games. The meter (`src/core/league/jobSecurity.ts`) reads where his standing would land if the season ends as it projects, counted in as the season is played (none of it on opening night, all of it at the review). A coach the review could fire whose season projects near or under the firing line at the All-Star break gets an ultimatum, a wins total or a postseason level still within reach: met, it buys back some standing; missed, he's fired whatever else went right. After the playoffs each goal is graded from −100 to +100 by how far you beat or missed it (5 a win, ±100 at 20; a postseason goal +30 reached and 30 more each round beyond, −35 each round short; 10 for every $4M of profit; a payroll line kept +15 to +30, crossed −25 to −100 at 5% of the tax line over; minutes ±100 at half the target), the weights make the season's grade and the standing is its running average (the first season sets it, each one after counts 40% on Normal, 28% on Easy and 52% on Hard; its wins, playoffs, money and development parts too; his reviews can't fire you in your first two seasons in charge on Normal, three on Easy, one on Hard), and each review goes to the inbox goal by goal and into the history (`League.ownerReviews`, ten seasons). With job security on, a standing under −50 after the three-season grace period gets you fired (a fired coach has to take over another team before the league moves on): resampling those seasons, an average roster's coach is fired within ten seasons 1.2% of the time, while one 20 wins under his roster (and out of the postseason, with the gate it costs) stands near −45 after two such seasons (already under the firing line three times in ten, though the grace period keeps him) and under −50 after a third four times in five. Taking over another team in season brings goals from that day, prorated to the games left (in the playoffs, the next round); in the summer, on opening night. A save from before goals gets them from the next day of a season under way.
- **Coaching carousel** (`src/core/league/coaches.ts`): AI owners review their coaches after the playoffs too, never in season and never the user's team. A coach with two full seasons in charge (one for the league's first coaches, at half the odds) is fired with a chance that rises with how far his team fell short of what its roster should have done over the last season or two (its win% against the league's line through the minutes-weighted OVR of the players who played, each 0.08 a step, the last season counting 60%) and with a record under .400, more for a contender out by the first round, less after a title in the last three seasons, and 0.5-2.5% after a strong season (.600 or the conference finals): about four changes a summer (3.7-4.3 over twelve simulated seasons, real and fictional; the NBA averages about six counting midseason firings), seven in ten of them coaches with a losing record. Openings go to the best of the former head coaches out of work (judged on their records shrunk toward .450, titles, Coaches of the Year and playoff runs of the last six seasons; five seasons without a job and they retire from the pool) and two new candidates an opening with new names (no two head coaches in a league's history share one, nor a name the user went by before renaming himself), the better jobs choosing first: about two in five go to former head coaches. A new coach brings his own profile (roots, development, adjustments) and fits his own playbook to the roster, and every firing and hiring makes the news ("The Bulls fired Joe Smith after 4 seasons (140-188).", "The Bulls hired Ann Lee, 2 titles with BOS."). It all draws on a random stream of its own, so the league's own draws are the same with or without it. When the user takes over another team the two coaches swap jobs, each with his own profile (a fired user keeps his job on the books until he does), and Settings won't rename him to a name another coach has had.
- **Job market** (`src/core/league/jobs.ts`): once the carousel has made its moves, the teams that changed coaches look at the user too, judged as the carousel judges its candidates. A fired user hears from every opening whose owner rates him no more than 0.25 below the coach it just hired (a proven coach let go this summer counts for less) and always from one at least; with no openings at all, the two owners likeliest to make a change offer to make one for him. A user still in his job, after two seasons in charge, is approached by up to two openings that rate him 0.4 or more above their hire. His agent mails the offers (an important message, so long sims stop for it), Office → Career lists them above the other teams with each team's strategy, payroll and record, and each can be taken or turned down until the draft, when they lapse. Taking one is a takeover like any other (the coach in charge there takes the user's old job) and makes the news as a hiring ("The Bulls hired Joe Smith (180-148 in 4 seasons, 1 title); Ann Lee takes over the Pistons."); a fired user can still take any team he likes. None of it draws from a random stream.

**Simplified CBA**

- **Cap and tax**: a soft cap (free agents need cap space unless signed for the minimum), a luxury tax paid by brackets of 3.68% of the cap over the line (1.5×, 1.75×, 2.5×, 3.25×, then 0.5 more a bracket; a repeater, a taxpayer in three of the last four seasons, pays 1 more on every bracket; half of the pot is shared among the teams that didn't pay), two aprons (126.7% and 134.4% of the cap, the 2025-26 ratios) and a salary floor. The mid-level exception (once a season, up to 8.5% of the cap for up to 4 years) and the bi-annual (3.3%, up to 2 years, not two seasons running and not with the room exception) hard-cap the team at the first apron for the season (no trade can take it past); a team the mid-level would take past the first apron gets the taxpayer mid-level instead (3.7%, 2 years, hard cap at the second apron). A team that signs a free agent with cap space loses the mid-level for that season and gets a room exception instead (5.7% of the cap, up to 2 years, once). Over the first apron after a trade a team takes back no more salary than it sends; over the second it can't combine salaries to take back a bigger one, send cash, use a traded player exception or trade its first in the furthest draft on the books. The cap sheet marks the aprons and shows the bill by its brackets, a hard cap and the open traded player exceptions. AI teams use exceptions too, the smallest that fits first, and an offer sheet reserves its exception until it's decided (one on cap space holds the space, and the mid-level is gone meanwhile). They mind the tax by strategy (a team out of contention most) and keep payrolls to budgets: a contender its core to the first apron and a star to the second, a young star (86+, 28 or under) up to 10% of the cap past it on any team. Over four seasons on three seeds about 11 teams paid the tax at the end of a season (9-13 after the first, whose books are the real 2026-27 ones: 14-16), 2-3 of them over the second apron.
- **Contracts**: re-signing your own players on Bird rights (full after three seasons with the team, a trade keeping the clock: up to his max, five years, 8% raises; Early Bird after two: up to 175% of his salary or 105% of the league's average; after one: 120% of his salary; anyone else signs for four years at most, with 5% raises), with cap holds on them until you re-sign or renounce them or free agency opens. The max is 25% of the cap up to six seasons in the league, 30% for seven to nine and 35% from ten; with All-NBA, MVP or Defensive Player of the Year honors his own team can pay a player off his rookie deal 30% (the Rose rule) and one with seven to nine seasons 35% (the supermax), and a player worth 95% of his max or more asks the max of every team, so where he signs is up to how much he likes each. Salaries follow value, steepest at the top (about 25% of the cap at 85 and 35% at 90; role players a little less); a star of 30-33 keeps most of his value in his ask, and a veteran takes up to 20% less from a contender. Over twelve simulated seasons that keeps 30-37 deals of 25% of the cap or more and 15-19 max deals. Player and team options are decided after the draft (you choose on your own team options), first-round picks sign on the rookie scale, and a release leaves dead money (minimum deals signed for the coming season aren't guaranteed until opening night). A release or buyout of a player with seasons left can stretch the dead money evenly over twice the seasons left plus one (in season this season is paid as usual and the rest stretches from next season; in the summer all of it does): the Release confirmation shows both schedules, the cap sheet and Finances label stretched dead money by season, a waiver claim still takes it off the books, the log notes "stretched", and AI teams never stretch. Young players can be qualified instead of re-signed: see Restricted free agency.
- **Extensions**: in the final season of a player's deal you can extend him, any time from the re-sign period through the regular season (not a deal signed this season, a two-way deal, or a player with an option still to decide). The extension starts when his current deal ends, runs up to four years (five coming off a rookie-scale deal) with 8% raises, his own team's, and counts on that season's cap like a re-signing. He asks about his market value in that season's money (about 5% less coming off his rookie deal or at 31+, 5% more as a young star, moved by his interest in your team); a star on a team he doesn't fancy may want to test free agency, giving the same answer every time you ask that season. An extension shows everywhere the contract does (payroll, the cap sheet and contract tables with EXT, expiring lists, cap holds, dead money, trade value, "Extended through" on the player page), and like a signing it means he can't be traded for a while. AI teams extend about 60% of their willing 76+ players on opening night (85% of their 84+ ones) within their re-signing budgets, and a preseason mail lists your candidates. Extension talks are a negotiation like any other (see Contract talks).
- **Rosters**: 13-15 players in season (up to 21 in the offseason, free agency included; draft picks can take you past 21, and then signings, re-signings included, and player-adding trades wait until you cut back; after the season only next season's contracts count, for two-way slots and two-way conversions too), plus up to three two-way contracts outside the roster and the cap. From January 5 to the end of the regular season a free agent who'd take the minimum can sign a 10-day contract (`src/core/league/tenDay.ts`): his minimum prorated, paid when he signs, ending by itself with no dead money. A team can give a player two a season (a stint with another team in between doesn't reset its count); then it signs him for the rest of the season or he leaves. With four or more players out for 3+ games, the hardship exception allows 10-day players beyond 15 (one for each player out past three). Free Agents has a 10-day mode, the roster, player page and Office show the days left, and the inbox asks on his last day: Second 10-day, Rest of season, or Let him go. AI teams sign 10-days to get back to 13 healthy players.
- **Trades**: salary matching when over the cap in the 2023 CBA's tiers (up to 200% of a small salary sent plus 0.16% of the cap, what's sent plus 4.85% of the cap in the middle, 125% plus the buffer above; no more than what's sent over the first apron), a traded player exception for a team that sends out more than it takes back (the difference, for a year, usable while under the first apron), protected draft picks (yours protected as you trade them), pick swaps, cash (about 5% of the cap each way a season) and the Stepien rule. Players on expiring deals can't be traded after the season, nor can a player whose option is undecided until he decides after the draft. A signing can't be traded until December 15, or three months after an in-season signing; a team can't take back a player or pick it traded away the same season, not even through another team; and a team hard-capped by an exception can't take its payroll past its apron in a trade. A contract's surplus or burden in trade value is counted season by season over up to four seasons still to be paid, extension years included, in today's money as the cap grows.
- **Free agency**: free agents can be signed in preseason, the regular season and free agency, and unsigned free agents lower their price every few days, down to what a team that wants them can still pay, until someone bites. A player whose contract runs out, or whose rights you renounce, joins the market like a released one (an asking price, the price-cut clock, and free of his old team's trade-back rule and trade lock). The summer market opens for every team at once, AI teams making a first wave of signings that day, though nobody spends cap space on anyone but the top free agents until those have decided (over the first three days). Top free agents (worth 78+ or asking 8% of the cap or more) weigh offers instead of taking the first one at their price: submit at least 90% of his ask and a day or two later he picks between yours and the AI teams' by the money against what he asks each team, how much he likes each team and the contract length, and the inbox says who he chose and why. Each player has his own priorities (winning, money, playing time, market) weighting how much he likes a team; across the league they average out, so prices hold steady. A contender bids up to 10% over a top free agent's ask, and his old team can bring him back on his Bird rights over the cap within its budget; a star holds his price for two weeks, then comes down to 60% of his value, and from day 25 takes what's left (one no team can pay 60% of his value takes the best money there is once the first three days are over, rather than the minimum a month in); one worth 78+ still unsigned when the market closes goes back to his old team on a deal of two years at most. A team never signs a player it released that season. Over four seasons on three seeds 54% of AI teams' expiring players rated 80+ stayed (44% before these rules), about 15 of 30 second-rounders were stashed on two-way deals, and AI deals carried some 40 player options a season. The offer sheet shows his decision day, who else is bidding, whether your offer leads (and about what would) and how your team fits what matters to him; pending offers hold their cap space, mid-level and roster spot until he decides (one on cap space ends the mid-level while it's pending, as using the space would, just as one on the mid-level keeps the space shut), and you can raise or withdraw them.
- **Contract talks** (`src/core/league/negotiation.ts`): re-signing, free agency, offer sheets and extensions are a short negotiation with the player's agent. His ask is shown, and offering it on his terms signs him at once. He may take a little less, how much he keeps to himself: up to 12% under the ask, less for a team he likes, later in the summer market and for older players, more for money-first players and stars, the same answer every time that season. Below his price he counters between your offer and his ask on the length he wants, and Accept counter takes it. He hears three offers below his price (one more if he's keen on your team, one fewer for a star); a lowball (under 85% of his ask) counts twice and costs some of his interest in your team. When talks break down, in the re-sign period his ask goes up 5% and he takes nothing less; in free agency he won't talk for three days, or the rest of the summer after two lowballs; in season for a week; about an extension for 30 days. Each year off his preferred length costs 3%; a player option on the final season takes 4% off, a team option adds 6%. AI teams sign at the ask, and top free agents still weigh competing offers.
- **Restricted free agency** (`src/core/league/rfa.ts`): in the re-sign period you can extend a qualifying offer to a young player whose deal is up: a first-round pick finishing his rookie-scale deal, or anyone with three seasons or fewer counting the one just played (not a two-way player, nor one coming off a qualifying offer). It is one season at 130% (the #1 pick) to 150% (#30) of a rookie-scale final salary, 125% for anyone else, and at least the minimum plus $0.2M. When the market opens he is a restricted free agent. His team can still re-sign him with Bird rights until he signs an offer sheet, and its cap hold for him (the larger of the offer and his ask) stays on its books through free agency, in trades too, until he signs or the offer is withdrawn. Another team can sign him to an offer sheet: 2-4 years, no options, at what he asks of it, paid for with cap space, the mid-level or the minimum, which stay held until his team decides. His team has two days to match and keep him on exactly those terms (matching needs a roster spot, not cap room), or he joins the other team. Free agency stops when an AI team signs one of your restricted free agents to a sheet; the inbox, the Free Agents screen and the Office offer Match or Let him go, an unanswered sheet lets him go at its deadline, and the new season waits while one is pending. Other teams' restricted free agents show "RFA · BOS can match" and sign offer sheets instead of contracts, and the inbox says whether their team matched and why. A restricted free agent still unsigned when the market closes plays a season on his qualifying offer (if his team has a roster spot) and is unrestricted after it. AI teams qualify the young players worth 66+ they let go (when he's worth at least 80% of the offer on the market), sign restricted free agents worth 72+ to sheets a little more reluctantly than unrestricted ones and one at a time, match when the contract is worth having and fits what they'll pay to keep a young player (their re-signing budget, or up to 5% past the tax), and come to terms with their own after ten days without a sheet. Top free agents who are restricted don't take bids: offer sheets replace them.
- **Waivers and buyouts**: a player released during the regular season with guaranteed money left spends two days on waivers; teams claim in reverse order of record (the AI when the contract is worth having and it has a roster spot and the cap room, or it's a minimum deal), a claim takes over the contract and takes the dead money off the old team's books, and you can claim from the Free Agents screen. Claims close with the regular season, as signings do: anyone still on waivers then clears. Preseason cuts, minimum camp deals and buyouts skip waivers. Veterans (28+) can be bought out from the Release confirmation: they give back up to 22% of what they're owed (more when older or on a team that isn't contending), which comes off the dead money. The day after the trade deadline, AI teams out of contention with a full roster buy out a role veteran or two (30 or older, his deal up this season or next, outside the team's six best, under 82), and each signs for the minimum with the contender he likes best: about five a season. Released players, bought out or waived, can't re-sign with the team that let them go that season, AI fills included.

**NBA Cup** (`src/core/league/cup.ts`): each season the teams are drawn into six groups of five from pots by last season's record, and each team plays four group games on Cup nights in November (Tuesdays and Fridays, the last one the day after Thanksgiving). The three group winners and the best runner-up in each conference reach the December knockouts: quarterfinals and semifinals at the better seed, then a championship on a neutral floor. Group, quarterfinal and semifinal games count in the standings; the championship counts in neither the standings nor season stats. As in the NBA, 80 games per team are scheduled up front with the knockout window kept clear, and once the groups are decided the teams that didn't advance (and quarterfinal losers) get their last games there, so everyone plays 82. Group ties break on head-to-head, point differential and points scored in group games, then last season's record. The League hub has a Cup tab (group tables, bracket, championship); the winner, runner-up and Cup MVP go into awards and history.

**Trading** (`src/core/league/trades.ts`, `proposals.ts`, `deadline.ts`): the trade dock gives the other GM's two main reasons under the value meter (a veteran worth less to a rebuilder, picks that count extra for a rebuilder or don't help a contender win now, salary that puts them into the tax, players they'd have to waive, depth that doesn't add up to talent, an asset that changed hands this season counting at face value, the two-firsts limit), and warns you when you'd give up far more league-neutral value than you get even if they say yes. When they turn a proposal down, a sheet offers up to three versions they would sign, best for you first: something more from you, one of their pieces left out, or one of their bad contracts for you to absorb. The Find tab lists every player AI teams can trade, filtered by position, OVR, age and the salary you can take back, with each player's value tier (Franchise, All-Star, Starter, Rotation, Depth, or Negative for a contract worth less than nothing) and whether his team looks ready to move him or builds around him; tapping one opens the builder with their ask. AI teams also call with real offers: a team that wants one of your players sends its best package for him, a rebuilder offers a veteran, a taxpayer attaches picks to a contract it wants off its books when free agency opens and you have room, and on draft night teams offer to move up into your pick. Offers come about once every ten days in season and every third day before the deadline, at most four at once and one per team, each standing five to seven days, or until you've seen it (an unread offer stands up to three weeks, so a long sim doesn't let it lapse unseen; the deadline-day mail stops a sim while offers wait), with no player in two open offers at once and the pitch worded from the team's record and its thin spot; each is a deal the team accepts and the rules allow, never more than 10% against you in neutral value, accepted from the inbox or the Trade screen's Offers tab (or countered in the builder), and drawn on its own random stream so it never changes a game. Players you mark untouchable are never asked for, in offers or in "what would make this work?". AI-to-AI deals (`aiTrades.ts`) keep the NBA's calendar: a trickle before December 15 (summer signings can't move until then), more into January and again from mid-January, about two a day in the deadline's last week and a rush on deadline day. Most are sales: a rebuilder's veterans, a retooling team's older players and a star on an expiring deal who won't extend are shopped (the seller's best three, to every contender, and to retooling teams for a 76+ player 30 or younger) and the deal the seller likes best is taken, up to four players a side, so two- and three-for-one consolidations happen. The rest are salary dumps, picks attached, by teams over the tax line (the player sent back has to make the salaries match; when no single deal gets a team under the line, one that saves at least 3% of the cap still goes; a retooling team protects only its best five); a team out of contention over it, or a contender past the second apron, also tries on the summer market's third day, in the preseason, a week before the deadline and on deadline day. An AI team a deal takes past 15 waives the end of its bench (up to three, the cheapest to let go first, dead money counted). Teams trade on draft night (moving up into a first, buying a second), over the summer from the market's third day and in the preseason. Over four seasons on three seeds (42, 7, 99): about 40 AI-to-AI trades a season (32-46), 30 of them in the regular season (46% in its last week) and 11 on draft night, in the summer and in the preseason, with a player rated 87+ moving about once a season. Weekly rumors run in the last three weeks, with a deadline-day briefing and a recap of the season's trades the day after. With "Stop before every pick" the draft waits before each team's pick, so you can trade for the pick on the clock or take offers for yours.

**Deal terms** (`src/ui/trade/Terms.tsx`): a team can protect its own first as it trades it (top 4, 8, 10, 14 or 20: if it lands there it stays home and nothing more is owed; a pick already protected, or another team's, keeps its terms), give another team the right to swap firsts in a future draft (both must hold their own unprotected firsts of that draft; the right rides on the taker's first, goes with it if it's traded, and is settled when the lottery sets the order: if the other first landed higher the two change hands, in the news and the transaction log), and send cash (each team sends and takes up to about 5% of the cap a season, $8.3M in 2026-27; cash isn't salary, comes off the sender's profit and onto the receiver's, outside revenue sharing). AI GMs value a protected pick by the slots it still conveys in, a swap right by its expected gain over both picks' projected slots through their pick lens, and cash at about what second-round picks sell for ($2-3M buys a typical one; rebuilders a little more, contenders less). Labels name the terms everywhere ("2028 1st (NYK) · top-10 prot.", "2029 1st · ATL may swap"), the deal card and dock say what each does ("stays yours about 23% of the time; ATL count it 41% lower"), "What would make this work?" closes a small gap with a little cash before a pick or a player, and a turned-down deal can come back without your protection or with cash added. AI teams never use these terms.

**Transaction log** (`src/core/league/transactions.ts`): every trade (what each side sent), signing and re-signing, extension, release and waiver, claim, buyout, two-way deal and conversion, renounced rights, option decision (yours, and the player opt-outs that make the news), qualifying offer (extended, withdrawn or taken), offer sheet and match, exercised pick swap and draft pick (the first round and yours) is logged with the player's name and the pick labels as they were that day, so it reads the same after he retires or the pick is made. AI teams' quiet roster fills and minimum camp deals aren't listed. Trades and every move of your team stay for good, even after you move to another job; other teams' moves are dropped after two seasons (about 19 KB a season, 55 KB in all). A trade line names swap rights and cash ("for a 2028 1st · top-10 prot., the right to swap 2029 firsts and $1.0M in cash"). Each team page has a Moves tab (by season, the summer as its own block, filtered by trades, signings, releases or other), a player's page has a Transactions card ("Acquired by NYK from BOS with a 2027 1st for …", "Extended by BOS: 4 yrs / $112.0M"), and the Trade screen's partner card lists your last three deals with that team.

**God mode** (`src/core/league/godMode.ts`, `/god`): an in-game editor, off by default and turned on or off at any time (Settings → God mode, or the hub's switch); edits made stay when it's off. While it's on, a wand in the top bar opens the hub, every player's and team's page has Edit, and the Trade screen has Force. The player editor sets his ratings (each on the 40-99 scale, or all his skills at once to a chosen OVR, with the OVR, potential and archetype they'd give him shown as you go), development, bio (name, position, number, age, height with his size rating, weight, country, college), health (an injury and its games out, healing, wear, injury proneness), team (another team takes his contract with him as in a trade; free agency leaves no dead money; a free agent who joins a team signs his asking deal) and contract (this contract season's salary and length with the usual raises, an option, a two-way deal). The team editor sets its city, name, abbreviation and colors (its mark and its players' avatars follow), front-office strategy, cash, hype and its head coach's development and tactics ratings (the coach registry follows), and heals and rests everyone at a tap. New players are made to order (name or a random one, position, age, height, OVR, development) as free agents or onto a team, on a random stream of their own; Force puts a deal through whatever the other GM, the cap, the roster limits or the calendar say. An edit is checked whole before anything changes and applies whole or not at all: rules it bends are listed as warnings (a 16th player, a fourth two-way), and only what would break the league stops it (a team left with fewer than five players to dress in season, a duplicate abbreviation, a number a teammate wears or the team has retired, a prospect moved off the draft board, a pick swap on picks that aren't both teams' own open firsts). OVR, potential, archetype, tendencies, rotations, roles and jersey numbers follow at once; edits wait while a sim runs or a live game is in progress (it replays from its journal with the rosters it tipped off with), and Settings counts the league's edits (`League.godEdits`). The editor's sheets load the first time one opens, so with god mode off it costs the game nothing.

AI front offices pick a strategy by where their roster ranks (about ten contenders, ten rebuilders) and blend in their record as the season goes, so a contender at 10-30 starts selling before the deadline. They re-sign within a budget that fits that strategy (a young player worth 80+, 28 or under, can be kept up to the tax line, a contender's to the first apron, and any 80+ player up to 4% of the cap past it), bring back any of their own free agents on Bird rights once the market opens, give contenders the first look at the market each day, shop their veterans when rebuilding (26-27-year-olds too, all but their best) or fading (from 28), add their third-best player to land an 85+ star, qualify their young players and match offer sheets by value and budget, trade among themselves a couple of times a season (both sides have to like the deal, and nobody sends away more than two firsts in one trade), value consolidation and salary dumps honestly, put second-round picks on two-way deals, bring a clearly better free agent into summer camp even with a full roster, and make preseason cuts by value. A rebuilding team near the bottom of its conference turns to its youth (`core/ai/coachAI.ts`): from about its 20th game for a bottom-four rebuilder, after the deadline for any rebuilder 11th or lower, and halfway with 15 games left for any non-contender 12th or lower, its rotation puts players 22 and under first, plays its veterans less and rests them more, and two-way players get minutes. It costs such a team 2 to 3 points per 100, as tanking does, gives the league about three sub-25-win teams a season (one and a half before) and keeps the win spread near 12 over twelve seasons; the news says so once a season, and the user's team never does it. On casual difficulty their trade discount applies to your own players and picks, not ones you just traded for, so a chain of trades can't compound it. Their strategy lens only adds a premium for what they take in (for a bad contract coming in, at most 15% off its burden): an AI team never sells you its own players or picks below their neutral value, and anything that changed hands this season is valued at face value, so flipping a veteran from a rebuilder to a contender isn't free money. A pick's value moves smoothly toward its draft, and the pick on the clock is worth the prospect it will become. "What would make this work?" adds picks and players together, sends a player when your roster is full, and says whether salary or value was the problem.

stats.nba.com, ESPN and Basketball Reference block many cloud IPs, so everything except the Basketball Reference pages comes from GitHub-hosted mirrors or NBA.com's S3 bucket, which download from anywhere.

The projection needs a Dunks & Threes subscription, so nothing downloads it: the CSV is committed in `data/epm/projection/` (`loadProjection` in `scripts/data/epm-projection.ts` reads every `.csv` there and keeps the seed season's rows, a later file by name winning a player listed twice). Players are matched by the NBA.com id the play-by-play crosswalk already has, else by name and team. Without it (no folder, or no rows for the seed season) the build fails. The seed used to also take Dunks & Threes' EPM for each of the three stat seasons; those exports were dropped for the projection, which is built from them and says what a player is now, so the ratings come from Basketball Reference's per-game and advanced tables, the NBA.com play-by-play, and the projection.

The **projection** is Dunks & Threes' estimate of each player's 2026-27 season: three seasons of EPM and box score regressed by sample, aged a year, and the rookies' college numbers translated. The seed's ratings describe players as they are on opening night, and that is what it estimates, so `data:build` uses it once, as the second of the two targets every player is calibrated against in the engine (`scripts/data/calibrate.ts`, step 4 below): his style is his real line, his value is his projected EPM. Each calibration pass simulates a few hundred games per team, fits ridge-regressed plus-minus (RAPM, as EPM is built on) over the simulated stints, the offense's five and the defense's five on the floor for each (`scripts/data/value.ts`), and compares each player's simulated offensive and defensive value with his projected offensive and defensive EPM, its actual gaps between players included (the old rank mapping, which handed the seed's OVRs out in projected-EPM order, is gone). A miss moves only the skills whose evidence is his impact and whose part in the engine is what his teammates get rather than his own line: offensive IQ (feel, which lifts every shot the five take) at one end; defensive IQ and perimeter or interior defense with rim contests, by where he defends, at the other. What feel adds to his own scoring comes back out of his finishing, mid-range, threes and handling in the proportions that hold each zone's make rate and his creation where they were, so his level never spills into his scoring, and the style targets hold his line there. Value moves a skill at most 15 points (about one standard deviation of a rating) from where his evidence put it: the engine values some players far from their projection whatever their skills (a high-usage scorer's efficient volume above it, a non-scoring role player's below, a rim protector's blocks and boards above, a small guard's defense below), and closing those gaps entirely pushed feel and defense to the ends of the scale (Giannis's offensive IQ to 34, Smart's to 96). Players with thin samples lean on their draft slot and college production for their style (step 5), and take their value from the projection like everyone else; players it doesn't list (rostered, waived or abroad by Dunks & Threes' rosters) have no value target and are leveled by their minutes or draft slot. Of the seed's players, 466 are listed (46 rostered ones aren't). In the committed build, value moved offensive IQ 11 points on average from the evidence (197 rotation players reached the band), defensive IQ 8, perimeter defense 6.5, interior defense 5 and rim contests 3, with each skill's league mean held where the evidence put it, so value moves players only against each other and the style targets keep the league's level. The last pass misses by 0.9 points per 100 on offense and 0.6 on defense; measured afresh over 820 games a team, simulated value follows projected EPM at r 0.85 on offense and 0.76 on defense (0.79 and 0.69 in the seed built with the rank mapping). Over three `real-check` seasons each team's net rating follows its players' projected EPM at r 0.91 with a slope of 1.04 (0.90 and 0.91 with the rank mapping, 0.82 and 0.80 calibrated to style alone), the win spread is 13.2 (the NBA's is about 13) and the net-rating spread 6.0 (5.4-6.1). OVR, the fixed blend of ratings below, follows projected EPM at r 0.86 over the 311 players projected for 15+ minutes (the rank mapping forced 0.99), and the real league's raw OVR is spread wider than before (SD 5.5, from 3.9), mostly because the projection rates deep-bench players below the league average their regressed evidence puts them at. Each player keeps his projection, which his Stats tab shows during that season, and the seed's source notes it.

**Pinned inputs.** Every file the build reads (the Basketball Reference pages, the NBA.com and college archives, the extras and the projection) is pinned by its SHA-256 in `data/seed-inputs.json` (`scripts/data/inputs.ts`), and the seed records the pins' hash (`inputs`) and the day they were pinned (`generated`, also the extras' date). `data:build` checks the files against the pins before it starts and fails, naming each file added, changed or missing; the build is deterministic (seeded simulations, no clock), so a rebuild from the pinned files reproduces the seed byte for byte. New inputs are taken on purpose: `npm run data:build -- --update-lock` pins what is there and builds from it.

```bash
npm run data:fetch       # Basketball Reference, ~200 pages (drafts and careers back to 2003-04) at 1 request / 3.7 s, cached in data/raw/ (-- --refresh re-fetches contracts and current rosters)
npm run data:fetch-nba   # NBA.com play-by-play + matchups (3 seasons) and college box scores, ~90 MB
npm run data:fetch-extra # schedule, pick ownership, injuries, two-way contracts → data/raw/extra/ (-- --force to refresh)
npm run data:build       # check the pinned inputs → parse → profile → RAPM → derive → calibrate style and value in the engine → extras → src/data/seed.json (-- --update-lock pins new inputs first)
npm run data:extras      # refresh only the schedule / picks / injuries / two-way part of the current seed
npm run data:history     # apply the careers back to 2003-04 to a seed built without them (no recalibration; see below)
```

Or let GitHub do it: run the **Build seed data** workflow (Actions tab → Run workflow). It fetches everything on GitHub's runners, pins what it fetched (`--update-lock`), builds and calibrates the seed, verifies the app still builds and passes its tests, commits `src/data/seed.json` and `data/seed-inputs.json` to `main` and then runs CI, the Android APK build and the Pages deploy on it. It also keeps the Basketball Reference pages and the extras as a tarball on the `data-raw` branch, so later runs only fetch what's missing (plus the contracts and current rosters, which change all summer and are always fetched again), and a rebuild from that tarball, the same NBA.com and college archives and the committed projection reproduces the committed seed. Its options:

- **commit_seed** (on by default): untick it to only build the seed and upload it as the run's `seed` artifact. A seed that differs from the committed one only in its dates and input pins isn't committed either way.
- **extras_only**: just refresh the injuries, pick ownership, two-way contracts and schedule in the current seed (no recalibration).
- **refetch**: ignore the pages kept on `data-raw` and fetch all of them again.

If an extra source, or a required Basketball Reference page (contracts, per-game or advanced stats), can't be downloaded, the workflow fails and leaves the committed seed alone. Locally, `data:fetch-extra` only warns: `data:extras` then keeps the seed's current version of that part, and `data:build` falls back to the game's generated version (and, the files having changed, needs `--update-lock`).

The current seed has 544 players: 483 under contract for 2026-27 plus the 61 unsigned players who played 250+ minutes in 2025-26 (the build takes up to 140, most minutes first). 26 players are on two-way deals (23 of them were unsigned in the contracts table, so they join their two-way teams). Six rows of the contracts table are waived players their teams still pay (Ricky Rubio, Didi Louzada, Taj Gibson and three more): the game books that pay as dead money, and those who played in the seed's seasons start as free agents. All 802 NBA.com players from the last three seasons are matched to their Basketball Reference ids. The committed seed was built before the pipeline recorded undrafted players' entry classes, so 95 undrafted players arrive without draft data: the game counts their NBA seasons from their first season on record (VanVleet 10, the season he lost to injury included), treats undrafted 2026 rookies as rookies, and numbers second-round picks within the round. The pipeline now records each undrafted player's entry class, so a rebuilt seed carries the real values.

**Careers.** Ratings come from the last three seasons, but every player's real regular seasons go back to 2003-04 (LeBron James's rookie year, so everyone in the league has his whole NBA career): 1,661 earlier seasons for 310 players, from Basketball Reference's per-game and advanced tables (no shot locations or EPM that far back). They're the imported seasons on his player page and count in career totals, Compare, franchise leaders, career milestones (round totals of a veteran's whole career), retired numbers and the Hall of Fame, where his whole career replaces an estimate from his last three seasons (`preLeagueLegacy` in `season.ts`: each season's game score above replacement, squared and summed, so Westbrook's MVP years count). Old team codes go under today's franchises as Basketball Reference files them (Seattle under the Thunder, New Jersey under Brooklyn, the New Orleans Hornets under the Pelicans, the Bobcats under Charlotte), and League → Stats lists only the three seasons it has for everyone. For each franchise a player gave five seasons and left, the roster page of his last season there gives the number he wore, so it can go up when he retires (Durant's 35 in Oklahoma City, Giannis's 34 in Milwaukee): 129 numbers for 117 players, from 73 more roster pages that `data:fetch` picks once the stat pages are in (`scripts/data/careers.ts`). The committed seed took its careers with `npm run data:history` rather than a rebuild, since they never reach the ratings: the seasons, the numbers and LeBron's 2003 draft went in and nothing else moved, and the seed grew from 1.15 to 1.99 MB (258 to 411 KB gzipped). A league saved before keeps its three seasons and the game's estimates for them.

What's real on opening night, besides the rosters and ratings:

- **Schedule**: the NBA's 2026-27 calendar, from opening night (Oct 20: BOS@DET, PHI@NYK, OKC@SAS) to Apr 11, with the real All-Star break (the trade deadline is the Thursday ten days before the All-Star Game, as in generated seasons). Later seasons, and fictional leagues, use the generated schedule.
- **NBA Cup**: the real groups (East A: BKN, DET, MIL, ORL, TOR …) and their 60 group games on the real Cup nights (Oct 30 to Nov 27), with quarterfinals Dec 4-5, semifinals Dec 8 and the championship Dec 11. The knockout matchups and the two games for each team that doesn't reach them are decided by the simulated group stage, as the NBA will do. If a seed has no Cup data, the missing games go on open December dates instead and there is no Cup that season.
- **Draft picks**: RealGM's ownership rows for 2027-2033 (`scripts/data/extra/picks.ts`). Each pick gets exactly one owner. Pools ("less favorable of MIL and NOP") and swap rights are resolved on a projected draft order (team strength from the seed's ratings, regressed toward the middle for later years), and conditions that depend on other picks ("if DAL conveys its 2027 first", "if not already settled") are checked against picks already resolved. A pick that conveys only outside the top N ("3-30 to Charlotte") is stored as a top-N protected pick. Where several outcomes are possible, the most likely owner gets the pick. The build prints every team's first-rounders and compares them with LD Sport's table.
- **Injuries**: players listed out on opening night start injured. The time out is counted in their team's real games before the expected return date, and a return after the regular season costs playoff games too. ESPN's description is mapped to the game's injury types where one fits (Torn ACL, Hamstring Strain...), otherwise kept descriptive (Torn Labrum). Injuries are off when the league's injury setting is off.
- **Two-way contracts** are marked and don't count toward the 15-man roster.
- **Positions** come from each player's box-score position in his latest season (Basketball Reference's current roster pages only say G / F / C). Rookies without one get theirs from the G/F/C label, size and skills: a guard who handles and passes like a lead guard for his size is a PG, a forward who handles more and rebounds less is an SF.

How ratings are made (`scripts/data/`):

1. **Play-by-play profiles** (`nba/pbp.ts`): one pass over each season builds every player's shot diet by zone (the engine's zones: rim, paint, mid-range, corner three, above-the-break three) with make rates, how he creates (pull-ups, drives, cuts, post moves, putbacks, transition, share of makes assisted), assists by the zone they set up, turnover and foul types, fouls drawn, true rebound rates from on-court opportunities, and rim deterrence (opponents' rim attempts and rim FG% with him on the floor). It also rebuilds every lineup stint.
2. **RAPM** (`nba/rapm.ts`): ridge-regressed adjusted plus-minus over about 200,000 stints from three seasons (recency-weighted, home court modeled), split into offense and defense. Matchup data (`nba/matchups.ts`) adds points saved per 100 possessions guarded, relative to what the same scorers did against everyone else.
3. **Attributes** (`derive.ts`): seasons are weighted by recency and minutes (2025-26 ×1.0, 2024-25 ×0.6, 2023-24 ×0.3), with Bayesian priors on percentages. Finishing, floaters and mid-range come from zone make rates; handling from self-creation and lost-ball rate; passing from assist and bad-pass rates; rebounding from true rebound rates; defense from RAPM, matchup defense, rim deterrence, steals and blocks; offensive and defensive IQ from box plus-minus, RAPM, true shooting and usage. They are the evidence the calibration starts from: the projection enters only there, as the value target.
4. **Engine-in-the-loop calibration** (`calibrate.ts`): seasons are simulated repeatedly and each player is compared against two targets together. His style: his simulated profile against his real one, shooting ratings and hidden per-player tendencies nudged until he reproduces it. His value: what his team gains with him on the floor in the simulated games against his projected EPM, his feel and defense nudged within their band (see Real data above). Twelve passes on growing batches with shrinking steps, then four more after the small samples are leveled, so the noise in each batch's simulated RAPM averages out instead of walking the ratings. The tendencies cover usage, three-point rate, rim, mid-range and corner-three shares, self-creation, passing, turnovers, share of rebounds, steals, blocks and fouls. The tendencies absorb role and opportunity, so they don't distort the visible ratings. To keep them from overfitting one context, half the calibration games use next season's rosters and half the 2025-26 teams the stats came from, each game draws the coach's playbook from his top three, and players randomly miss games so rotations get re-planned around them.
5. **Rookies and small samples**: 2026 draftees are shaped by their college production (shooter, passer, rim protector...) and start at the level of their draft slot and reputation; under 400 NBA minutes, a player's derived ratings blend toward that prior, and under 300 his line isn't a style target. Those the projection lists then take their level from their value target; short NBA samples it doesn't list are shrunk toward the level their minutes suggest, and calibration settles again.
6. **OVR** is a fitted, size-aware blend of attributes. The weights were fitted so that real players' ratings predict half regressed box plus-minus and half RAPM, plus the minutes and usage coaches gave them. With the seed calibrated to the projection, OVR follows projected EPM at r 0.86, box plus-minus at 0.77 and RAPM at 0.73.

`npm run real-check` simulates a season with the real league and compares every regular with his real 2025-26 numbers. With the current seed, sim-vs-real correlations over about 228 regulars are 0.90 for points, 0.91 for assists, 0.93 for rebounds, 0.92 for blocks, 0.90 for threes made, 0.81 for steals, 0.82 for minutes per game and 0.71 for true shooting, and each team's net rating follows its players' projected EPM at r 0.91 (slope 1.04) and their real box plus-minus at r 0.91 (averages over 3 seeds). Minutes follow because coaches remember last season's roles, counted once and converted to full-strength minutes, so real rotations carry over without burying healthy veterans. Every league per-game rate (pace, points, threes, free throws, rebounds, turnovers, steals, blocks) lands within 6% of the 2025-26 NBA but assists (4-8% high), field-goal percentage runs 2% high (0.480), and shot diets match too: about 0.92 for rim share, 0.87 for mid-range share and 0.93 for three-point share. It also prints the game shape (margin spread, blowouts, overtime, win spread), each team's net rating against its players' real box plus-minus and EPM (and their projected EPM, for a seed built with it), the leaders, single-game highs and the DPOY ladder, so outliers are easy to spot.

The pipeline is covered by parser and pipeline unit tests (CSV, RAPM, name matching, the projection, simulated value, the value step and its band, the input pins), an offline end-to-end fixture and seed sanity tests. The fixture writes Basketball Reference-style pages (and an EPM projection) for a simulated fictional league, so the build (parse, derive, calibrate) runs offline in about a minute and a half:

```bash
npx tsx scripts/data/test/make-fixture.ts /tmp/fx && HC_RAW_DIR=/tmp/fx HC_SEED_OUT=/tmp/fx-seed.json npx tsx scripts/data/build-seed.ts --update-lock
```

The data scripts read a few environment variables: `HC_RAW_DIR` (where the raw downloads live, default `data/raw`; the NBA.com and extras folders inside it follow unless `HC_NBA_DIR` or `HC_EXTRA_DIR` point elsewhere), `HC_EPM_PROJ_DIR` (the projection, default `data/epm/projection`, or `epm/projection` inside `HC_RAW_DIR` when that is set), `HC_SEED_OUT` (the seed that `data:build` and `data:extras` write, default `src/data/seed.json`), `HC_INPUT_LOCK` (the input pins, default `data/seed-inputs.json`, or `seed-inputs.json` inside `HC_RAW_DIR` when that is set), `HC_REPORT` (`data:build` also writes a per-player JSON report there: minutes, advanced stats, RAPM, projected EPM, ratings and tendencies) and `HC_PICK_DEBUG` (a pick such as `2027:1:DAL`, whose competing ownership claims the picks resolver prints).

npx tsx scripts/engine-fingerprint.ts  # one hash of a fixed set of seeded games and a season slice (--verbose per part): a refactor must not move it

## Ship to phones

- **PWA**: deploy `dist/` to any static host and use "Add to Home Screen". It works offline after the first load, and saves stay on the device (IndexedDB, with persistent storage requested). Saves from before the tactics overhaul (save format 7) can't be opened, a clean break: the main menu lists them to export or delete. Saves are incremental (game results live in their own store and only new ones are written; older ratings snapshots keep just OVR and potential, so a long career grows by about 0.4 MB a season), a live-game journal that's damaged or isn't for the game still to be played today is dropped on load, a failed save says so, offers a backup and keeps retrying (a brand-new league included), opening another league, importing a backup or starting a new one saves the open league first (and offers an export when it can't), and a league open in two windows can't overwrite itself. A new version downloads in the background and goes in the next time the app is left (never during a sim, a live game or a new league's setup, and only once the league is saved); reloading after an update or from the error screen saves the league first.
- **Backups**: Settings → Export backup saves the league as a JSON file (the share sheet in the app, a download on the web). Import it from Settings or the start screen on any device.
- **Android**: the _Android APK_ workflow builds a debug APK on every push to `main` (download it from the run's artifacts). Pushing a `v*` tag attaches the APK to a release. Locally: `npm run cap:sync && npx cap open android`. The native shell adds the hardware Back button (closes the topmost sheet first, goes Home when there is no earlier screen, saves before leaving), haptics, edge-to-edge insets and no service worker (the files ship in the APK).
- **Android signing**: Android only installs an update signed with the same key as the installed app. Without signing secrets, each workflow run signs with a throwaway debug key, so a new build only installs after uninstalling the old one, which deletes its saves (export a backup first). To sign every build with one key, create a keystore once and keep it safe (a lost key means users must reinstall):
  ```bash
  keytool -genkeypair -v -keystore release.keystore -alias hoops -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000
  base64 -w0 release.keystore   # paste the output into ANDROID_KEYSTORE_BASE64
  ```
  Then add four repository secrets (Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions): `ANDROID_KEYSTORE_BASE64`, `ANDROID_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD`, `ANDROID_KEY_ALIAS` (`hoops` above) and `ANDROID_KEY_PASSWORD`. Debug APKs are then signed with that key, and `v*` tags also get a signed release APK (`app-release.apk`) on the release. The version code is the workflow's run number, so each build counts as newer than the last.
- **iOS**: `npm run cap:sync && npx cap open ios` on a Mac with Xcode.
- **GitHub Pages**: the _Deploy PWA to Pages_ workflow publishes every push to `main` (and runs by hand from the Actions tab), once Settings → Pages → Source is set to "GitHub Actions". It builds for the site's own path (`/<repo>/` for a project site, `/` for a user site or a custom domain). Pages on a private repo needs a paid GitHub plan.

## Design decisions

- Ratings are shown on a 2K-style 40-99 scale, fully visible. OVR is anchored to the league's distribution, so 93+ is a superstar, 89+ All-NBA, 87+ an All-Star, 82+ a quality starter, 78+ a starter, 72+ a rotation player and 67+ a bench player, with about 74 the median rostered player. Each tier has one badge colour (gold, purple, blue, green, light grey, grey) and every list of badges carries the key; attribute ratings and skill percentiles use the same colour order. Attributes use the same 40-99 scale (league median ≈ 68).
- The contracts table includes camp deals, so some teams start with 16-20 players. AI teams make preseason cuts down to 15 standard contracts at league creation (two-way contracts don't count). If your team is over, the Assistant GM suggests who to release before opening night.
- Difficulty is one of three presets (`src/core/league/difficulty.ts`), Normal by default: Easy takes deals about 12% under value and has your players ask 10% less, with no opposing game plans and a patient owner; Hard wants a 12% premium, asks 12% more, has every coach game-plan you and an impatient owner. Settings shows them side by side.
- Future picks start with their real owners (2027-2033). The game's pick model is one owner per pick plus an optional top-N protection and swap right, so RealGM's conditions are simplified (seed swaps are still resolved on the projected order): swaps and "more/less favorable of" pools go to the projected outcome, protections that send the pick to a third team, or roll over to a later year, are approximated as "stays with the original team that year, obligation ends", and picks the NBA has forfeited stay with their original team.
- The real schedule is used for 2026-27 only. Games the NBA hasn't published yet are filled on open December dates.
- Team logos are original monograms in each franchise's colors (no trademarked artwork).
