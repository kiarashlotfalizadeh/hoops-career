# Hoops Career

A mobile-first basketball GM **and** head-coach game, built as an installable offline PWA with Capacitor shells for Android and iOS. It starts in the 2026-27 season with real rosters (New League also offers a fictional league with the real franchises), and its main feature is a tactics layer that tells you _why_ things happen.

## What's in the box

**Possession-by-possession engine** (`src/core/sim/engine.ts`, its parts in `src/core/sim/engine/`: one module per part of a game, from the pick-and-roll to rebounds, fouls and substitutions)

- Every half-court trip runs a concrete action: pick-and-roll, isolation, post-up, handoffs/off-ball screens, or motion/cuts/drive-and-kick. Transition, putbacks, presses and end-of-game situations are modeled too.
- Defense chooses its pick-and-roll coverage (drop, switch, hedge, blitz, ice), base scheme (man, 2-3, 3-2), help level, ball pressure, physicality, transition defense, press and a star plan (deny or double). The 3-2 zone contests threes harder than man-to-man but is softer in the paint, post, cuts and glass, so it beats the 2-3 against shooting teams and loses to post-up and drive teams; switching off the ball denies handoffs, but switched handoffs and cross-screens hand out mismatch isolations and post-ups where the smaller defender fouls. A box-and-one (four in a 2-2 zone, one chaser) or triangle-and-two (three in a zone, two chasers) face-guards their main creators: fewer touches, late catches, a defense that loads up on him sooner, few catch-and-shoot looks and a tiring chase, while a defender caught high gives up drives on the catch and back-cuts, and everyone else gets more open threes, gaps and offensive boards. Named for players in a game plan or live, it plays man-to-man while they sit. Against a star with non-shooters around him a named box-and-one is worth about +1.5 points per 100 possessions; against a shooting team about −4.6.
- Each tactical effect is tagged with its expected-points value (e.g. "drop coverage conceded pull-ups: +3.4"). That is what drives the post-game report and debrief, the live bench's calls and what it says each of your moves did.
- Rotations follow a minute-by-minute stint plan built from your depth chart. The staff overrides it for fouls, fatigue, injuries and blowouts (hard minute caps hold: a capped player sits down up to 40 seconds early, since subs wait for a dead ball), and playoff rotations shorten to about eight (the starters and closers always among them). The starters open both halves, and the closers finish the game; the coach's starting and closing fives take the floor whatever their positions. A fouled-out or injured player is replaced by whoever the staff wants on the floor at that point, so he isn't subbed straight back out. A reserve who starts for an injured or resting starter takes over that starter's minutes. Garbage time empties the bench in real blowouts only: the starters come straight back if the game gets within reach, and nobody subs in a whole new five in the last half minute. You can also sub manually during live games.
- NBA rules at the edges of the game: teams get a shot off before the buzzer (a team tied or down three or less gets one off with a second or two left; a team protecting a late 4th-quarter lead runs out the clock instead), the shot clock runs from the inbound (breaking a press uses some of it) and a side-out foul restarts it at 14 or more, a timeout stops play (the trip out of it is an inbound, never a run-out) and in a live ball only the team with the ball can call one, hack-a-player stops in the last two minutes of every period, trailing teams foul only while a foul can still buy another possession (never down three; down four to six from the last 30 seconds), a team down three late hunts a three and one down two plays for the tie, at most two timeouts after the 3:00 mark of the 4th, and short-handed teams never play four (a player with six fouls stays in when nobody is left, and each foul from his sixth on adds a technical; an injured player with no healthy sub is replaced by the last player disqualified). Box scores add up to exactly 48 minutes a team (plus 5 per overtime), and the starters are the five who took the floor for the tip.
- AI coaches adjust during games: at quarter breaks and timeouts, how often they change coverage, help, pressure or the glass depends on their adjustments rating. Your assistant can do the same in your simmed games (Game Mgmt), and game reports list both benches' adjustments by quarter.
- Calibrated to 2025-26 league averages: pace ≈ 100, ORtg ≈ 114, ~41% of shots from three, about 27 assists per team game with NBA-like assisted shares, and turnover, rebounding, foul and block rates within 5%. Games vary like the NBA's (a margin SD near 13 for a given matchup, about 16% decided by 20+, about 4% to overtime, the two scores moving together), and team quality spreads the standings: team net ratings track what the players' real box plus-minus (BPM) predicts at the minutes they get (r ≈ 0.86), and win SD is about 11 (the NBA's is about 12). Tactic extremes have real costs, so no slider setting is a free win. Tactics matter: every matchup edge (a coverage against the action it faces, a zone against a shooting or a post team, a junk defense, a set against the defense it beats, a guarding instruction, a star plan, pressure against loose or secure handlers) is played at a tactical leverage of 2 (`K.tacticEdge`), so the gap between the right call and the wrong one is about twice what it was (right calls about +0.8 points per 100 on average, wrong ones about −1.2), while a setting chosen blind is worth no more than about 1.5 and the league's averages stay where they were. `scripts/tactic-impact.ts` measures each aimed choice with paired seeds. A full season sims in about 5 to 6 s on a laptop (a game is about 1.35 ms in the engine), in a Web Worker (`src/workers/sim.worker.ts`), so the screens stay responsive and show the league as it stands every 20 days.

**Coaching & understanding**

- **Tactics** (Team → Tactics: System, Offense, Defense, Players, Game Mgmt, Insights): the System tab opens on the **system report** (`src/core/analysis/system.ts`): seven things your system should be good at on each end (shot creation, spacing, rim pressure, ball movement, transition, second chances, ball security; rim protection, perimeter D, pick-and-roll D, three-point D, the defensive glass, transition D, forcing turnovers), each graded A+ to F by where it ranks against the other 29 teams' systems (their rotations and tactics), and each a tap from what it's made of: the players who carry it (minutes-weighted, the best one counted extra where one man carries it) and what every setting adds or takes away, in the directions the engine plays them (help packs the paint and leaves shooters, a 3-2 zone guards the arc and gives up the glass, crashing the glass leaks run-outs, a drop needs a rim protector, a switch needs defenders of a size, pressure forces turnovers). The Offense and Defense tabs carry the same grades in a strip over their settings that lights up as one moves ("Second chances D → A+", "Transition D A → C"), and the changes dock says what the visit's edits did to them, so every lever shows what it buys and what it costs. Under the report: your five on a half court where their roles put them, each with how well he suits his role (★ to ★★★★★, `roleFits`; the role sheet shows every role's stars, and a player out of his best role says which it is), what the season's games say is working and hurting (in points a game, from the engine's tags; Insights has all of it, and how other teams read you), the playbooks to start from with their fit for your roster, and My playbooks. Every 0-100 setting is picked from five named levels (pace: Grind it out, Patient, Balanced, Up-tempo, Run & gun; each action from Almost never to Heavily) and explained in place, each explanation behind a "?". A fit is a starting score plus what each roster quality adds or takes away (lead creator, shooting, a passing big, rim protection, depth...), and each list sorts best fit first with the fit as a figure and a bar. Each playbook's fit is fitted to what it measured in paired engine games for rosters like yours (a fit point is about 0.1 points per 100; a coach's own philosophy adds 3, enough to break near-ties), so the best fit is the one the games reward, and the assistant picks by it: letting the assistant manage beats the old fit-by-feel picks by about 2.3 points per 100 over 210 rosters. When the staff runs your tactics it also acts on the season's own evidence: a setting whose trade-off the games say costs more than it wins back is changed (and stays changed until its cost has halved), and the lead assistant mails what he changed and what it was costing; for tactics you run yourself the same evidence is a staff card with its one-tap change. The staff's rotation plays about ten and a half players, no stint under six minutes, and a starter it would play under 22 minutes swaps with the reserve who'd play eight more. Tapping a playbook opens its sheet: what it is, its fit and rank, the starting score and what each roster quality adds or takes away (they add up to the fit), the players behind each, what would raise it, and Use. Offense and Defense open on the playbook each starts from ("3 settings moved from it", with Put back), and a dot marks the playbook's own setting on every control. Presets, the staff's in-game adjustments and situational plans can land between the levels: the level nearest the value is the one shown, and the engine plays the value itself.
- **Situational plans and crunch time**: up to four situational plans (Team → Tactics → Game Mgmt) change your tactics while a situation lasts (a quarter range, time left, the score, or the second unit on the floor): the first enabled plan that matches plays on top of your base tactics until the situation ends, and the play-by-play says so. Live edits always go to the base tactics. A go-to player gets the ball much more in the last two minutes of a close fourth quarter or overtime (with none named, the best scorer on the floor does), and an after-timeout play opens your next half-court trip after your timeouts. Game plans can name hack-a-player targets and the quarter hacking starts, and a player hacked twice in a period goes to the bench when a reserve his size can come in. Foul to give (Game Mgmt) fouls the ball handler at once when the other team has the ball with about 3 to 8 seconds left in a period and a foul is only a side out (in the 4th and overtime only when tied or up by three or less), so they inbound with less time and a worse first look; about one in twenty is called on the shot. AI teams use none of these, so the league's numbers are unchanged.
- **Sets** (`src/core/sim/sets.ts`): your offense can run seven named sets: Spain pick-and-roll, Horns, Floppy, Elevator doors, Hammer, Chicago and Post split. Each is run for the player on the floor who fits it best (or one you name) and goes for its own look: the roll, an elbow touch or a pop, a curl or a catch-and-shoot three, the corner kick, the split cut. Nothing in a set makes anyone shoot better: each has edges against the defenses it's built to beat (Spain against drop and hedge, Floppy and Chicago against fighting over screens, Hammer against heavy help) and none, or worse, against the ones that kill it (switching, zones, staying home), and takes a second or two more of the shot clock. Defenses read sets: each run in a game makes its look a little less open and its passes easier to jump, sooner against coaches rated high at tactics, so a set is strongest early (a first run is worth about +0.1 to +0.3 points per possession against the defense it beats; each read costs about 0.1 to 0.25). Team → Tactics → Offense keeps a package of up to four that your half court mixes in on fresh trips (never in late-game situations, only for players who fit, and your staff stops running one once the defense is on it) and any set can be your after-timeout play, run for a player you name. A package is a small edge, not a free one: about +0.45 points per 100 possessions at best against a drop, about −0.2 against switching (paired engine games, `scripts/tactic-impact.ts`). Game and period reports list each set's runs, points per possession and the run that got it read. AI coaches run no sets, so their games play exactly as before.
- **Saved playbooks** (`src/core/sim/playbooks.ts`): "My playbooks" keeps up to twelve named playbooks of your own (schemes, every slider, your set package, game management, and roles, shot freedom and screeners by player, which only load for players still on your roster; matchups, star, chase and hunt targets and instructions by player are game by game and aren't saved). Each shows what loading it would change ("Pace 62 → 80", "Pick-and-roll coverage Drop → Hedge") and can be loaded, overwritten, renamed or deleted; the header names the playbook your tactics came from, with "· edited" once they move from it. The library is the coach's, so it comes along when you take over another team.
- **Game plans** (`src/core/league/gamePlans.ts`, the Plan tab of each opponent's matchup page, `/games/:tid`): a plan for each opponent, for the next game against them, the current playoff series or the rest of the season. Its items replace one another by kind (one ball-screen coverage, one star plan...) and change actions relative to your playbook, so they build on whatever you run; matchups can be planned too. The Plan tab sets the scope, lists the items with the evidence behind them and the assistant's counters to add (Add for this opponent, Always for every game), sets a star plan on any of their top eight, and the Matchups tab plans matchups: their projected top eight against your top ten, with the staff's tip-off assignment (from the engine's own matchup solver, `src/core/sim/matchups.ts`) and what each pairing means (size gap, his creation against your defender's perimeter D). A plan can also set a junk defense and who it chases, how to guard each of their top eight (`src/core/analysis/guarding.ts`: go under screens, top-lock him off the line, sag off him, get physical; each changes only the actions he's in, with the staff's ★ suggestion from his games or his ratings), and which of their defenders to attack (your isolations, post-ups and ball screens go at the man he guards, which costs some ball movement; a coach being hunted with success hides him on your weakest scorer). The Matchups tab and the live player sheet set the same instructions, and none of it is kept beyond that opponent. The assistant suggests a box-and-one or triangle-and-two against a one- or two-man offense whose others can't shoot, and attacking a real weak link, with the numbers.
- **Scouting from the games played** (`src/core/analysis/scouting.ts`): each opponent's matchup page (its Scout tab) reads its actual games: pace, offensive and defensive ratings, the four factors at both ends, three-point and rim rates with league ranks, play types at both ends with points per possession against the league's (and how unusual each is), key players with on/off numbers and your meetings with them, read into plain-English tendencies and ways to attack them (last season's numbers, labeled, until a team has played five games, without play types: of last season's results the save keeps only your games). The assistant's counters are drafted against the plan you already have for that opponent, and those the numbers back carry that evidence and come first (sprint back against a transition team, defend without fouling against a team that lives at the line, press after makes against loose handlers, post-ups against a weak post defense...).
- **Opposing game plans** (`src/core/ai/opponentPlan.ts`): AI coaches scout you too. On normal a coach comes in with a plan for about half your games (more often the better he is at tactics), on hard every coach does, and on casual none do. A plan is up to two changes to his usual tactics, each answering something your games show (a hedge or ice against a pick-and-roll you score well from, a drop against hot rollers when he has a rim protector, staying home on a three-point team or packing the paint against one that can't shoot, sprinting back, defending without fouling, ball pressure on loose handlers, running against a team that doesn't get back, denying or doubling the player you run your offense through, or a box-and-one on him when the rest of your team doesn't make its threes) or exposes something in yours (attacking your weakest perimeter defender when he gives up a lot to the rest of your rotation, or guarding one of your top seven the way your own staff would read him: going under on a handler who can't pull up, top-locking a shooter who can't drive, sagging off a non-shooter, getting physical with a poor free-throw shooter inside). The matchup page says "Expect from them tonight: ices ball screens, denies Brunson the ball" (with the numbers behind each item), the preview plays their plan exactly as the game will, the live period report lists it, and the report's Why tab keeps "Their game plan". Whether he plans, and what, comes from the game's id and your games before game day, so it never touches the league's random stream and AI-vs-AI games are unchanged.
- **Pre-game** (the matchup page's Preview tab, `src/core/analysis/preview.ts`): Watch & coach (the Continue bar on game day) opens tonight's matchup page on its Preview first, with Tip off and Quick sim in its dock (Settings → Pre-game screen turns this off; Watch & coach live in the Play options always goes straight to the game). It opens on the staff's briefing: the lead assistant's read of them (who to watch and why), what they run on offense and defense and where to hit them (the scouting report), and his plan for tonight (the counters the numbers back first, one per kind), each a tap into your game plan, or Use the staff's plan for all of them. The plan is held to what measures well in paired games (following it is worth about +1 point per 100 and +3 points of win%): he never suggests a zone (it stays on the Defense tab), doubles only a star who can't pass out of it with non-shooters around him, and offers a box-and-one or triangle-and-two only against a cast that can't shoot by its ratings, not just a cold month. AI coaches plan against you the same way. Then both teams' records, conference ranks, form and ratings with league ranks, back-to-backs and playoff stakes ("R1 · Game 5", elimination games); a projection (this season's net ratings, weighted toward what each rotation is worth tonight until enough games are in, plus about 1.8 points of home court, as the engine plays it: home teams win about 54.7%); both injury reports (out, resting, worn down) and both projected starting fives as the game will start them; the opponent's play types against the league's, its defense and how often its coach adjusts; and their coach's plan for tonight; who guards whom and your game plan are the next tabs over. Storylines (`src/core/analysis/storylines.ts`) set up the night: a player facing a team he played for ("for the first time since he left" only when every game since is stored), a career milestone one good night away, team streaks and a player's run of 20-point nights, last season's playoff rematch, the MVP race and two No. 1 picks. The injury report makes the game-time decisions: a player one game from back from a minor or moderate injury (not a concussion) is questionable and sits unless you clear him, and cleared he plays about 6% below his best and twice as likely to get hurt in the game, with the game counting as the one he had left (the starters, the projection and a resumed live game all play it that way; other teams always sit theirs). Today's next-game card says when it is and lists who is out on both sides, the series, whether a plan is set and the lead assistant's one line on them (who to watch, where to hit them), and the Continue bar names the opponent ("vs BOS (12-8) · today"). Keys to the game (`src/core/analysis/keys.ts`) give you three numbers to hit tonight, read from both teams' games before today: what they do best to take away (keep them off the offensive glass, run them off the three-point line, defend without fouling, get back in transition, protect the paint), what their defense does to teams (take care of the ball against one that forces turnovers; crash the glass, get to the line or let it fly against one that gives those up), making the scorer they run it through work, and sharing the ball. They're picked strongest signal first (standard deviations from the league's teams, never two on one number) and each target is about where tonight's number lands if both teams play to form (their own and what the other allows, halved), so a little under half are met (the offensive glass is read as a share of the misses, since a count grows with a team's own misses); over three teams' seasons, the games with none met were won 31% of the time, one 40%, two 63% and all three 78%. In a later season's first five games they read last season's team totals (its results keep only your games); on a new league's opening nights, with no games to read, they come from the other coach's tactics and his players' last season. Because they read only the games before tonight, the preview, the live game and the recorded result all have the same three.
- **Live game**: an animated 2D court driven by the engine's possession logs, with a bench under it that says what's happening and lets you act on it. The court is the home team's floor (its colours in the paint and in an apron round the court, its mark at center court and its name behind the basket; a neutral floor for the Cup final), and the scorebug gives each team a block in its colours (the away side wears its second colour when the two would read as one). It's laid out like a broadcast: a slim scorebug over a full-width court, the console under it, and the bench bar in the tab bar's place until the final (Timeout, with the ones left, then the console's views: Bench, Game plan, Lineup and Stats; the scorebug carries the counts: Timeouts, Fouls, Penalty in the bonus, and To win). "More bench" at the top of the console makes the court smaller on a phone for more of it; held sideways, the court takes the left side and the scorebug, the bar and the console the right. The **Bench** (`src/ui/live/staff.ts`) is the lead assistant's (a name of his own on each team you run, `leadAssistant`): his read of the game in a sentence (the biggest edges each way in the box score), then the calls he wants from you, each with its evidence and the moves on offer, his pick first: a run the staff won't stop (Timeout), tired legs and foul trouble when you make the subs or in crunch time (the sub), their hot scorer and who's been on him (your best perimeter defender on him, deny him or double him), a play type of theirs running hot (its counter: "drop is giving their handlers room", Hedge), the biggest tactical edge against you (its one-tap fix), the defender of theirs you're scoring on (Attack him), your own actions stalling or working (lean away from them or into them), and the late-game fouls when they're off and matter. Who's scoring on whom comes from the possession logs: each trip is credited to the player who finished it and the defender on him. Under the calls: the scorer of theirs he's watching, the keys to the game as they stand, and **your moves** (`src/core/analysis/moves.ts`): every tactics change, sub and timeout you made, in the Tactics screen's words, with the scoring while it was in effect against the stretch before it ("Pick-and-roll coverage: Drop → Hedge · SAS pick-and-roll 0.81 a trip since (11), 1.33 before"; a sub over the stint it started, a timeout over the trips either side of it), called Working, Not working, No change yet or Too early by how far the rate moved against the noise of the trips behind it. "Let the staff make tactics calls" (there, or Settings → Staff makes tactics calls) hands him the tactics: he makes each change he would bring you himself, once a game each, and it shows in your moves as his; subs and timeouts stay yours. The **Game plan** puts each of tonight's numbers beside the lever that moves it: their play types (points a trip against the league's, coloured by which way is good for you) with the ball-screen coverage beside their pick-and-roll, help beside their spot-ups, cuts and post-ups, transition defense beside their run-outs and pressure beside their isolations; their scorers with who's been on each (a tap opens his sheet: deny, double, go under, top-lock, sag off, body up, a Hack-a, or attack the man he guards); your own play types with the action behind each; the defenders of theirs you're scoring on, with Attack; then the scheme, the pace and the press, tonight's plan and what changed since the tip (Reset), and Every setting (the Defense, Offense and Late game levers, the quick changes and "Keep changes after this game", which also keeps what you changed before turning it on and never saves tonight's game plan or matchups). **Lineup** has both fives with energy, fouls, points and who's hot ("4 straight makes") or cold, and the bench (tap one of yours for his sheet: the sub, his shot freedom and role tonight, go-to in crunch time and who he guards); **Stats** the box score (Team, Flow with the win-probability line, Shots by team, period and shooter) and the play-by-play (everything, the scoring plays or one team's). The breaks are on the screen itself. At a period's end the court steps aside for the **period report**: the staff's read and his calls for the next period, what's deciding it (each edge in points of the margin, with the fix where there is one), who's tired or in foul trouble going into it with the sub, the keys so far (each one's number, on pace for a full game, and On track, Behind, Done or Missed), the score by quarter, points a trip by play for both teams, the teams side by side and the scorers, their coach's plan and adjustments, and your moves so far. A timeout brings up the **huddle** the same way: the score, the clock and your chance to win, the staff's top call, who's tired or in foul trouble with the sub, the keys and your after-timeout play. Start Q3 or Resume is on the bench bar, and the other views are a tap away to change things first. One timeout called while they have a live ball (after a steal or a defensive board) is taken at the next dead ball, and none can be called at a period's buzzer. Games stop at every quarter, at halftime only, or never (Settings); a tie after regulation breaks as Overtime. Playback sits over the court like a video's, a tap away: pause, skip ahead (the end of the quarter, halftime, clutch time with 5:00 left in the fourth, or the final), the speed (1×/2×/4×, and the one you pick sticks) and Key plays (it draws only crunch time, the ends of periods, runs, timeouts, anything the staff flags and plays that put up a graphic, and fast-forwards the rest). The court calls each play by name as it draws it (`captionAt` in `src/ui/live/choreo.ts`: "Holmgren screens for Mitchell: they drop", "Mitchell finds Williams", "Williams, catch-and-shoot three…", "good! (Mitchell assist)", "Blocked by Wembanyama!", "And one! Foul on Castle"), with the engine's own shot calls (the step-back three, the alley-oop, the putback dunk the play-by-play names) and, in the last two minutes of the fourth or overtime, a make that ties it or takes the lead ("OKC lead 101-100!"); the ball handler stands in a pool of his team's light with his name under him, a shooter on a streak wears a heat ring, the team with the ball is named on the baseline it attacks, the ball spins with its seams and leaves a trail on a shot with its shadow shrinking under it, and the net sways on a make and the rim rattles on a miss (none of the motion with reduced motion on). A make as the clock runs out flashes BUZZER BEATER, or GAME WINNER from behind or level in the fourth or overtime, felt on a phone. A game from its tip opens with the starting lineups on the court (the arena, or a neutral floor for the Cup final, what's at stake, both fives with their numbers, positions and season lines) until you tip it off or a few seconds pass (Settings → Starting lineups at tip-off). Around the league (`src/ui/live/scoreboard.ts`, Settings → Scores around the league): the night's other games tick along with yours on a bottom line under the scorebug, each tipping at its home team's local 7:00 or 7:30 and running on the same broadcast clock (breaks and a fifteen-minute halftime included), read from its quarters, since the day's other games are played before yours; the ones that matter come first (teams within three places of yours once ten games are in, your next opponent, every playoff and play-in game), one that matters comes up as it goes final, and a tap opens every score. Everything on screen (scorebug, strip, alerts, box score, play-by-play) shows the game as the court has drawn it, never the possession still being drawn. Win probability (`src/core/analysis/winProb.ts`) rides under the scorebug (Settings → Win probability in live games): the chance to win from the score, the clock, who has the ball and the pre-game projection. It treats what's left as a normal margin, with the share of a lead engine games give back early (about 15% with 19 minutes or more left) and a spread that never quite closes while time is left, and is fitted on engine games (on 1,231 held-out games: log loss 0.459 against 0.501 for the margin alone, every 10% bucket within 2 points); a game in progress never reads 0% or 100%. Broadcast graphics go up over the backcourt now and then (a big quarter, one man scoring his team's last nine, perfect shooting, 30/40/50-point nights, a double-double or triple-double, a new season high or one within reach late, a career milestone, a franchise or league scoring record from the record book or one within reach late, a team run of 10-0 or more, a lopsided team number at the breaks): at most one every two game minutes, each once, only the big ones in the last 24 seconds of a period, and only what the drawn game shows. The scorebug adds team fouls (lit in the penalty), runs and the playoff series or Cup stage; each timeout flashes once, naming the team, and one banner at a time over the court reports injuries, foul-outs, the other coach's adjustments and the staff's word. A live game survives the app being closed: it's journaled in the save (`League.live`: how it tipped off and every sub, tactics change and timeout, with the possession it came after), brought up to date at breaks, on pause, with every move and when the app is hidden, and played back on reopening ("Picked up where you left off: Q3 4:12"). The engine draws every possession from the game's seed and possession counts, so the replay is the same game and must land on the recorded score and clock; a journal that doesn't is dropped and the game starts again from tip-off, with a note. Until the game is played, the Continue bar and the Play options, Today, the matchup page and the front office (Inbox trade offers included) treat it as a game in progress.
- **Post-game debrief** (on the live final card and on the Debrief tab your game reports open on, `src/core/analysis/debrief.ts`): the lead assistant's verdict, the three edges that decided it (each in points of the margin), your moves in a live game with what each did (and how many were working by the final), and what to do next time: the one-tap fix for each edge that went against you while your tactics still give it (the counter to their offense's edge, or your offense's answer to their defense's: more handoffs against a drop, motion against a zone, threes against their help, crashing the glass), except a setting's cost that the same setting more than won back that night ("Your drop coverage won 3.1 back"), and a standing cost isn't repeated after the next game unless it decided that one, for your plan against this opponent when you meet again or for every game, and a word on each key to the game you missed. Then how the keys to the game went (kept with the result: "2 of 3", each with its number on the night; a scorer who sat out doesn't count); the three stars (the player of the game first, from both teams); win probability's turning point (the play that swung it most the winner's way, named from the play-by-play), a comeback ("won after falling to 6%") or a lead let slip; round career thousands reached that night; your conference rank and games back before and after (kept with the result); newly clinched spots, your streak or the series and its next game; what was said after it: quotes from your player and the other coach (`src/core/analysis/quotes.ts`: templates for the margin, overtime, big nights, streaks, upsets, comebacks and collapses, injuries and what decided it, picked by the game's id so a game always reads the same); the night's results that matter to you; and your next game. Also four factors, play-type PPP, your sets (runs, points per possession, the run that got each read), lineups, player grades, shot charts, score flow with win probability (your team above, the three biggest swings marked and named, from the projection the game kept at tip-off), momentum swings (when, and from what score) and play-by-play by period.

**League systems**

- **Calendar**: the real 2026-27 schedule (82 games, 41 at home; a Cup quarterfinal or semifinal host plays one more at home, its opponent one fewer); later seasons and fictional leagues get a generated NBA-format one with no four games in five nights, no games on Thanksgiving or Christmas Eve, the All-Star Game on a Sunday and the trade deadline the Thursday ten days before it. Playoff games are every other day with breaks between rounds, so the Finals end in June; the summer has dates too (the draft in late June, free agency from June 30).
- **Competition**: the NBA Cup, standings with NBA tiebreakers (multi-team ties broken as a group; once the regular season is over every seed is marked), play-in and 2-2-1-1-1 playoffs. The news log keeps about a year of results, awards and transactions; injuries make the news from 10 games out (5 for a star). Career milestones make the news too (every 5,000 points; 5,000 rebounds or assists and every 2,500 after), counted only for careers the game has whole: a real veteran's three seasons on record aren't his career, so his aren't claimed.
- **Season forecast** (`src/core/analysis/odds.ts`, League → Standings → Odds, one tap from the conference tables): the rest of the season is played out 1,000 times, game by game on the real schedule, then the play-in, the 2-2-1-1-1 playoffs and the lottery's drawing, from each team's strength as the pre-game projection reads it (this season's net rating regressed toward what its roster is worth, an injured player counted for the games he'll be back for), itself uncertain (by 2.5 points per 100 before a game is played, less as the games come in). Each team gets its projected record with the 10th-90th percentile of wins and its chances of the No. 1 seed, the top six, the play-in, the playoffs, each round and the title; the Lottery view gives each team's own first-round pick its chances at No. 1 and the top four and where it lands on average (with its owner when it was traded). It reads only the league, each simulated season on a stream of its own seeded from the league and the day, so every screen shows the same forecast and the league's draws never change; 100% and "—" mean clinched or out (as the standings' clinch marks say), anything else rounds to >99% or <1%. Today, Office and every team page carry the team's line ("Projected 48-34 · Playoff odds 72% (+8 this week) · Title 3%", in the playoffs "Series vs BOS: 58% to win it"), the pre-game screen gives a regular-season game's stakes ("Playoff odds with a win 71% · with a loss 58%", from the same simulated seasons), and the user's playoff and title odds are kept a point a week (`League.oddsTrail`, under 0.5 KB) for a chart on the Odds view. Over twelve simulated seasons (forecasts from opening night to day 140), teams given 10-25% made the playoffs 18% of the time, 25-40% 39%, 60-75% 66% and 75-90% 84% (a Brier score of 0.113); a midseason forecast takes about 0.1 s on a laptop.
- **Honors**: All-Star selection by the NBA format (at least four guards and six frontcourt players per conference) and game, full awards (MVP, DPOY, ROY, 6MOY, MIP, COY, All-NBA, All-Defense, All-Rookie, Finals MVP; the 65-game rule, 50 games for Rookie and Sixth Man of the Year, Most Improved only for players with last season's numbers to improve on (and a real role in both: 12 minutes a game before, a rotation spot now; the jump, the level reached and youth count), the MVP weighing his team's wins twice (more again under 49 wins), the Defensive Player of the Year's team-defense credit capped and heavy scoring counted against him, and the League hub's award ladders use the same rules as the vote), history and Hall of Fame. Franchises retire their legends' numbers (`src/core/league/jerseys.ts`): when a player retires, each team he gave five seasons (a seeded player's real seasons count) and a career it remembers raises the number he wore there: his main team for a Hall of Famer, or any with an MVP or Finals MVP, a title with two All-NBA selections, three All-NBA or five All-Star selections there, or its scoring record in this league (15,000 points at least). In a real league the stars it opens with go up at the franchises they made their names with, their careers there on record (LeBron James's 23 in Cleveland, Durant's 35 and Westbrook's 0 in Oklahoma City): about seven numbers in a league's first four seasons, two a season through its twelfth, then three or so a season as the next generation retires; each is in the news (and the GM's inbox for his own team) and hangs as a banner in History's Franchise view and on the team page's History tab. A player already out when the coaches vote (about seven games before the game) who won't be back for it isn't picked, and an All-Star hurt after the vote is replaced only when the injury keeps him out more than two games. All-Star Saturday (`src/core/league/allStarSaturday.ts`) comes first that day. The Three-Point Contest invites eight healthy shooters by threes made and percentage this season (2.5 attempts a team game at least; a couple of All-Stars when they're among the twenty best) to the NBA's 27-ball round (five racks of four and a money ball, one rack all money balls at his favorite spot, two deep threes: 40 points at most), the top three to a final and ties to a shoot-off rack; each shot's chance comes from his three-point rating and a little of his form, money balls and deep shots harder and legs tiring late in a round, so first rounds average about 21 and the winning final about 26 (21-31 for 80% of winners). The Slam Dunk Contest invites four young high-flyers who aren't stars (26 or younger first, by bounce, speed, finishing and size; no All-Stars, nobody 85+): two dunks a round, five judges' 6-10s each (30-50) from his bounce, the size of the dunk and the crowd, one in ten not finished (30-36), the top two to a final and ties to a dunk-off, each dunk named for its score ("a 360 windmill", "a dunk from the free-throw line" for a 50). The day draws on a random stream of its own, so it never changes a game. The Awards tab shows both contests round by round (every dunk with its score and name), History lists the winners, and the winners get a chip after All-Star MVP (a token in the honors that rank careers, nothing toward the Hall of Fame), the news, and the inbox when one of your players took part. League → History → Coaches keeps every head coach the league has had (`League.coaches`, about 150 bytes a coach: his profile and his jobs, about 7.5 KB eight seasons in): the user's résumé job by job (seasons, W-L, playoffs, titles), the coaches in charge (seasons with the team, career W-L and win%, titles, Coaches of the Year) and the former ones, available or retired, each a tap from his jobs season by season. A coach's record is read from his teams' stored seasons: a job taken over mid-season counts only the games from that day (and one left mid-season only those before), and a season's playoff run goes to whoever had the team when its season was over. Coach of the Year goes to the coach it named (by his name then), or to whoever had the team at the end of the regular season. The team page's coaching staff card says "In charge since 2027-28 · 120-126 · 1 title" and names the coaches before him; a save from before the registry starts it from the coaches in charge (the user from his current job's first season).
- **Record book** (`src/core/league/records.ts`, League → History → Records): the best single games since the league began, by a player (points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, threes) and by a team (points, the biggest win, threes), the league's top ten (five in the playoffs) and each franchise's top three (its best in the playoffs), kept as each game is recorded (results older than last season are pruned, so they couldn't be read back later; about 40 KB in all, with a player's name written in only once he leaves the save). The regular season (Cup group games, quarterfinals and semifinals included) and the postseason (play-in and playoffs) keep separate books; the Cup championship and the All-Star Game count in neither, as in the stats, and a record tied stays with whoever set it first. Single-season records (points, rebounds, assists, steals and blocks per game with the NBA's 70%-of-games rule, total points and threes, and the shooting percentages with 300 field goals, 82 threes or 125 free throws made) and career leaders come from the stats every player keeps for good (a retiree whose season stands in those lists is never compacted away), and team seasons (best and worst records, net, offensive and defensive ratings, points per game) from the teams' finished seasons: only seasons played in this league count, never a seeded player's real ones. Each player's page has his career highs (labeled "since 2026-27" unless his whole career is in the book; about 0.15 KB a player). Once the book has a season behind it, league records, a franchise's scoring record of 45 or more and your team's main records make the news (routine news, pruned before trades and signings, since the book keeps them), your team's reach your inbox too, and the post-game recap names the records a game holds. A save from before the book reads it back from this season's results, the only ones it still has whole, and the book starts there. A game's records are its best marks against each list as it stood before the game, and a list with nothing on it yet (a franchise's first postseason) only starts.
- **Player movement**: the 2019-odds draft lottery (teams on the same record split the odds of the places they share, and pick in the reverse of their tie-break draw in the second round), generated draft classes with combine data, a mock draft (`src/core/league/mockDraft.ts`, the Draft screen's Mock view: the draft played out 250 times the way AI teams pick, on a stream of its own, in the order by record until the lottery and the real one after it, each pick named once with how often he was the pick and the next likeliest; the user's picks follow his big board or his staff, and each prospect on the board gets his range, where he went in four of five mocks, with his chance to be gone before the user's next pick; over five simulated drafts the real pick fell inside that range 82% of the time), re-signing and extensions, a 30-day free agency with competing offers for the top free agents, contract talks with counters and restricted free agency (qualifying offers, offer sheets and matching), 10-day contracts, waivers, buyouts and stretched releases, and trades (validation, AI valuation with its reasons, counter-offers, a trade finder, "what would make this work?", AI offers you can accept, draft-day trades, and terms beyond players and picks: protections you set, pick swaps and cash).
- **Players**: injuries and load management (worn-down players get hurt more; assistant-run playbooks refit when a long injury starts or ends), and development (players peak around 26, a strong skill fades faster with age than a weak one, young players follow a hidden development path to 25 that their potential doesn't show, so a late pick now and then becomes a star, and summers vary: about ±2.5-3 OVR a year in a player's prime; the league's spread is held by eight quantiles of each team's fifteen best, so the middle doesn't inflate; retirement odds rise from 36 and climb steeply past 38; training focus trades skills without costing OVR; coaching, practice intensity and minutes). Every player on a team wears his own number (an arrival whose number is taken, or retired there, picks a free one as he joins; a player wearing a number when it's retired keeps it until he leaves), no two active players share a name, and no two head coaches do.
- **Generated players** (draft classes and fictional leagues) get hidden tendencies like the real players' calibrated ones, drawn from how those relate to level and size, and every player's tendencies follow his level each offseason (a draftee who becomes a star takes a star's shots; an aging star hands them on), while a real player's calibrated usage settles toward his level's as his teammates change, so a long career keeps the real league's shot diet and spread: twelve seasons in, about 37 threes a game, a win SD near 12 and a scoring leader at 30-34. Generated players come in the real league's shares of each position. Fictional leagues are rated on the real league's spread and built with real rosters' heights by roster spot and age mix, so their players develop and age much like real ones (they peak about a year earlier, near 26).
- **Business**: finances that grow with the cap as salaries do, revenue sharing (each team pays or receives 40% of its gap to the league's average local revenue), and an owner who judges you against your roster (`src/core/league/ownerGoals.ts`, Office → Owner). On opening night, after the AI's cuts and signings, he sets three or four goals from the roster you open with, frozen for the season (`League.ownerGoals`): a win total (what its minutes-weighted OVR, played as an assistant would play it with each injured player back when he's due, should win against the league's average roster on the projection's net-rating line), a postseason goal from where that projects you in your conference and your front office's strategy (a contender or a top-four roster a playoff round, the conference finals when it's the conference's best by four projected wins and the Finals by nine; fifth to eighth the playoffs, ninth and tenth the play-in), for a rebuild or a roster projected 11th or worse a development goal instead (minutes for players 23 or under: what an assistant's rotation gives them, plus what young players earn as they improve; a retooling team that plays its young players gets one too), and a money goal: the season's profit on his budget (revenue and costs at today's ticket price, hype, market and payroll, revenue sharing, the salary-floor shortfall, a typical season's playoff gates and tax payouts, and the gate a winning roster adds or a losing one loses) or, with the payroll from 6% under the luxury tax to just over it (4% over for a frugal owner), staying or getting under it, and for a taxpayer beyond that keeping the payroll where it is. Each franchise's owner is a builder, win-now or frugal (a hash of the league and the team, never its random stream), which moves the targets (a win or two, a few million) and the weights a little, and a small market weighs money more. Over sixteen simulated league-seasons (two real and two fictional leagues, 480 team-seasons) the win targets were unbiased (actual wins 0.95 × his expectation + 2.0, r 0.72, a miss's SD 7 wins; within 1.7 wins on average in every band of expectation and for every front-office strategy that night), and every kind of goal grades near zero on average (wins +1, postseason +7, profit 0, payroll +3, development +1, the season +1 with an SD of 33). Office → Owner shows a job-security meter and each goal's progress (ahead, on track, behind, met or missed; the wins projected as the season forecast projects them, so it agrees with the odds line; in the preseason, the goals he'd set with today's roster), his personality, the standing and the past reviews; Today shows the meter when the seat is warm, and he writes at the All-Star break with every goal and once if the forecast has you five wins under his target after 40 games. The meter (`src/core/league/jobSecurity.ts`) reads where his standing would land if the season ends as it projects, counted in as the season is played (none of it on opening night, all of it at the review). A coach the review could fire whose season projects near or under the firing line at the All-Star break gets an ultimatum, a wins total or a postseason level still within reach: met, it buys back some standing; missed, he's fired whatever else went right. After the playoffs each goal is graded from −100 to +100 by how far you beat or missed it (5 a win, ±100 at 20; a postseason goal +30 reached and 30 more each round beyond, −35 each round short; 10 for every $4M of profit; a payroll line kept +15 to +30, crossed −25 to −100 at 5% of the tax line over; minutes ±100 at half the target), the weights make the season's grade and the standing is its running average (the first season sets it, each one after counts 40% on Normal, 28% on Easy and 52% on Hard; its wins, playoffs, money and development parts too; his reviews can't fire you in your first two seasons in charge on Normal, three on Easy, one on Hard), and each review goes to the inbox goal by goal and into the history (`League.ownerReviews`, ten seasons). With job security on, a standing under −50 after the three-season grace period gets you fired (a fired coach has to take over another team before the league moves on): resampling those seasons, an average roster's coach is fired within ten seasons 1.2% of the time, while one 20 wins under his roster (and out of the postseason, with the gate it costs) stands near −45 after two such seasons (already under the firing line three times in ten, though the grace period keeps him) and under −50 after a third four times in five. Taking over another team in season brings goals from that day, prorated to the games left (in the playoffs, the next round); in the summer, on opening night. A save from before goals gets them from the next day of a season under way.
- **Coaching carousel** (`src/core/league/coaches.ts`): AI owners review their coaches after the playoffs too, never in season and never the user's team. A coach with two full seasons in charge (one for the league's first coaches, at half the odds) is fired with a chance that rises with how far his team fell short of what its roster should have done over the last season or two (its win% against the league's line through the minutes-weighted OVR of the players who played, each 0.08 a step, the last season counting 60%) and with a record under .400, more for a contender out by the first round, less after a title in the last three seasons, and 0.5-2.5% after a strong season (.600 or the conference finals): about four changes a summer (3.7-4.3 over twelve simulated seasons, real and fictional; the NBA averages about six counting midseason firings), seven in ten of them coaches with a losing record. Openings go to the best of the former head coaches out of work (judged on their records shrunk toward .450, titles, Coaches of the Year and playoff runs of the last six seasons; five seasons without a job and they retire from the pool) and two new candidates an opening with new names (no two head coaches in a league's history share one, nor a name the user went by before renaming himself), the better jobs choosing first: about two in five go to former head coaches. A new coach brings his own profile (roots, development, adjustments) and fits his own playbook to the roster, and every firing and hiring makes the news ("The Bulls fired Joe Smith after 4 seasons (140-188).", "The Bulls hired Ann Lee, 2 titles with BOS."). It all draws on a random stream of its own, so the league's own draws are the same with or without it. When the user takes over another team the two coaches swap jobs, each with his own profile (a fired user keeps his job on the books until he does), and Settings won't rename him to a name another coach has had.
- **Job market** (`src/core/league/jobs.ts`): once the carousel has made its moves, the teams that changed coaches look at the user too, judged as the carousel judges its candidates. A fired user hears from every opening whose owner rates him no more than 0.25 below the coach it just hired (a proven coach let go this summer counts for less) and always from one at least; with no openings at all, the two owners likeliest to make a change offer to make one for him. A user still in his job, after two seasons in charge, is approached by up to two openings that rate him 0.4 or more above their hire. His agent mails the offers (an important message, so long sims stop for it), Office → Career lists them above the other teams with each team's strategy, payroll and record, and each can be taken or turned down until the draft, when they lapse. Taking one is a takeover like any other (the coach in charge there takes the user's old job) and makes the news as a hiring ("The Bulls hired Joe Smith (180-148 in 4 seasons, 1 title); Ann Lee takes over the Pistons."); a fired user can still take any team he likes. None of it draws from a random stream.

**Simplified CBA**

- **Cap and tax**: a soft cap (free agents need cap space unless signed for the minimum), a luxury tax paid by brackets of 3.68% of the cap over the line (1.5×, 1.75×, 2.5×, 3.25×, then 0.5 more a bracket; a repeater, a taxpayer in three of the last four seasons, pays 1 more on every bracket; half of the pot is shared among the teams that didn't pay), two aprons (126.7% and 134.4% of the cap, the 2025-26 ratios) and a salary floor. The mid-level exception (once a season, up to 8.5% of the cap for up to 4 years) and the bi-annual (3.3%, up to 2 years, not two seasons running and not with the room exception) hard-cap the team at the first apron for the season (no trade can take it past); a team the mid-level would take past the first apron gets the taxpayer mid-level instead (3.7%, 2 years, hard cap at the second apron). A team that signs a free agent with cap space loses the mid-level for that season and gets a room exception instead (5.7% of the cap, up to 2 years, once). Over the first apron after a trade a team takes back no more salary than it sends; over the second it can't combine salaries to take back a bigger one, send cash, use a traded player exception or trade its first in the furthest draft on the books. The cap sheet marks the aprons and shows the bill by its brackets, a hard cap and the open traded player exceptions. AI teams use exceptions too, the smallest that fits first, and an offer sheet reserves its exception until it's decided (one on cap space holds the space, and the mid-level is gone meanwhile). They mind the tax by strategy (a team out of contention most) and keep payrolls to budgets: a contender its core to the first apron and a star to the second, a young star (86+, 28 or under) up to 10% of the cap past it on any team. Over four seasons on three seeds about 11 teams paid the tax at the end of a season (9-13 after the first, whose books are the real 2026-27 ones: 14-16), 2-3 of them over the second apron.
- **Contracts**: re-signing your own players on Bird rights (full after three seasons with the team, a trade keeping the clock: up to his max, five years, 8% raises; Early Bird after two: up to 175% of his salary or 105% of the league's average; after one: 120% of his salary; anyone else signs for four years at most, with 5% raises), with cap holds on them until you re-sign or renounce them or free agency opens. The max is 25% of the cap up to six seasons in the league, 30% for seven to nine and 35% from ten; with All-NBA, MVP or Defensive Player of the Year honors his own team can pay a player off his rookie deal 30% (the Rose rule) and one with seven to nine seasons 35% (the supermax), and a player worth 95% of his max or more asks the max of every team, so where he signs is up to how much he likes each. Salaries follow value, steepest at the top (about 25% of the cap at 85 and 35% at 90; role players a little less); a star of 30-33 keeps most of his value in his ask, and a veteran takes up to 20% less from a contender. Over twelve simulated seasons that keeps 30-37 deals of 25% of the cap or more and 15-19 max deals. Player and team options are decided after the draft (you choose on your own team options), first-round picks sign on the rookie scale, and a release leaves dead money (minimum deals signed for the coming season aren't guaranteed until opening night). A release or buyout of a player with seasons left can stretch the dead money evenly over twice the seasons left plus one (in season this season is paid as usual and the rest stretches from next season; in the summer all of it does): the Release confirmation shows both schedules, the cap sheet and Finances label stretched dead money by season, a waiver claim still takes it off the books, the log notes "stretched", and AI teams never stretch. Young players can be qualified instead of re-signed: see Restricted free agency.
- **Extensions**: in the final season of a player's deal you can extend him, any time from the re-sign period through the regular season (not a deal signed this season, a two-way deal, or a player with an option still to decide). The extension starts when his current deal ends, runs up to four years (five coming off a rookie-scale deal) with 8% raises, his own team's, and counts on that season's cap like a re-signing. He asks about his market value in that season's money (about 5% less coming off his rookie deal or at 31+, 5% more as a young star, moved by his interest in your team); a star on a team he doesn't fancy may want to test free agency, giving the same answer every time you ask that season. An extension shows everywhere the contract does (payroll, the cap sheet and contract tables with EXT, expiring lists, cap holds, dead money, trade value, "Extended through" on the player page), and like a signing it means he can't be traded for a while. AI teams extend about 60% of their willing 76+ players on opening night (85% of their 84+ ones) within their re-signing budgets, and a preseason mail lists your candidates. Extension talks are a negotiation like any other (see Contract talks).
- **Rosters**: 13-15 players in season (up to 21 in the offseason, free agency included; draft picks can take you past 21, and then signings, re-signings included, and player-adding trades wait until you cut back; after the season only next season's contracts count, for two-way slots and two-way conversions too), plus up to three two-way contracts outside the roster and the cap. From January 5 to the end of the regular season a free agent who'd take the minimum can sign a 10-day contract (`src/core/league/tenDay.ts`): his minimum prorated, paid when he signs, ending by itself with no dead money. A team can give a player two a season (a stint with another team in between doesn't reset its count); then it signs him for the rest of the season or he leaves. With four or more players out for 3+ games, the hardship exception allows 10-day players beyond 15 (one for each player out past three). Free Agents has a 10-day mode, the roster, player page and Office show the days left, and the inbox asks on his last day: Second 10-day, Rest of season, or Let him go. AI teams sign 10-days to get back to 13 healthy players.
- **Trades**: salary matching when over the cap in the 2023 CBA's tiers (up to 200% of a small salary sent plus 0.16% of the cap, what's sent plus 4.85% of the cap in the middle, 125% plus the buffer above; no more than what's sent over the first apron), a traded player exception for a team that sends out more than it takes back (the difference, for a year, usable while under the first apron), protected draft picks (yours protected as you trade them), pick swaps, cash (about 5% of the cap each way a season) and the Stepien rule. Players on expiring deals can't be traded after the season, nor can a player whose option is undecided until he decides after the draft. A signing can't be traded until December 15, or three months after an in-season signing; a team can't take back a player or pick it traded away the same season, not even through another team; and a team hard-capped by an exception can't take its payroll past its apron in a trade. A contract's surplus or burden in trade value is counted season by season over up to four seasons still to be paid, extension years included, in today's money as the cap grows.
- **Free agency**: free agents can be signed in preseason, the regular season and free agency, and unsigned free agents lower their price every few days, down to what a team that wants them can still pay, until someone bites. A player whose contract runs out, or whose rights you renounce, joins the market like a released one (an asking price, the price-cut clock, and free of his old team's trade-back rule and trade lock). The summer market opens for every team at once, AI teams making a first wave of signings that day, though nobody spends cap space on anyone but the top free agents until those have decided (over the first three days). Top free agents (worth 78+ or asking 8% of the cap or more) weigh offers instead of taking the first one at their price: submit at least 90% of his ask and a day or two later he picks between yours and the AI teams' by the money against what he asks each team, how much he likes each team and the contract length, and the inbox says who he chose and why. Each player has his own priorities (winning, money, playing time, market) weighting how much he likes a team; across the league they average out, so prices hold steady. A contender bids up to 10% over a top free agent's ask, and his old team can bring him back on his Bird rights over the cap within its budget; a star holds his price for two weeks, then comes down to 60% of his value, and from day 25 takes what's left; one worth 78+ still unsigned when the market closes goes back to his old team on a deal of two years at most. A team never signs a player it released that season. Over four seasons on three seeds 54% of AI teams' expiring players rated 80+ stayed (44% before these rules), about 15 of 30 second-rounders were stashed on two-way deals, and AI deals carried some 40 player options a season. The offer sheet shows his decision day, who else is bidding, whether your offer leads (and about what would) and how your team fits what matters to him; pending offers hold their cap space, mid-level and roster spot until he decides (one on cap space ends the mid-level while it's pending, as using the space would, just as one on the mid-level keeps the space shut), and you can raise or withdraw them.
- **Contract talks** (`src/core/league/negotiation.ts`): re-signing, free agency, offer sheets and extensions are a short negotiation with the player's agent. His ask is shown, and offering it on his terms signs him at once. He may take a little less, how much he keeps to himself: up to 12% under the ask, less for a team he likes, later in the summer market and for older players, more for money-first players and stars, the same answer every time that season. Below his price he counters between your offer and his ask on the length he wants, and Accept counter takes it. He hears three offers below his price (one more if he's keen on your team, one fewer for a star); a lowball (under 85% of his ask) counts twice and costs some of his interest in your team. When talks break down, in the re-sign period his ask goes up 5% and he takes nothing less; in free agency he won't talk for three days, or the rest of the summer after two lowballs; in season for a week; about an extension for 30 days. Each year off his preferred length costs 3%; a player option on the final season takes 4% off, a team option adds 6%. AI teams sign at the ask, and top free agents still weigh competing offers.
- **Restricted free agency** (`src/core/league/rfa.ts`): in the re-sign period you can extend a qualifying offer to a young player whose deal is up: a first-round pick finishing his rookie-scale deal, or anyone with three seasons or fewer counting the one just played (not a two-way player, nor one coming off a qualifying offer). It is one season at 130% (the #1 pick) to 150% (#30) of a rookie-scale final salary, 125% for anyone else, and at least the minimum plus $0.2M. When the market opens he is a restricted free agent. His team can still re-sign him with Bird rights until he signs an offer sheet, and its cap hold for him (the larger of the offer and his ask) stays on its books through free agency, in trades too, until he signs or the offer is withdrawn. Another team can sign him to an offer sheet: 2-4 years, no options, at what he asks of it, paid for with cap space, the mid-level or the minimum, which stay held until his team decides. His team has two days to match and keep him on exactly those terms (matching needs a roster spot, not cap room), or he joins the other team. Free agency stops when an AI team signs one of your restricted free agents to a sheet; the inbox, the Free Agents screen and the Office offer Match or Let him go, an unanswered sheet lets him go at its deadline, and the new season waits while one is pending. Other teams' restricted free agents show "RFA · BOS can match" and sign offer sheets instead of contracts, and the inbox says whether their team matched and why. A restricted free agent still unsigned when the market closes plays a season on his qualifying offer (if his team has a roster spot) and is unrestricted after it. AI teams qualify the young players worth 66+ they let go (when he's worth at least 80% of the offer on the market), sign restricted free agents worth 72+ to sheets a little more reluctantly than unrestricted ones and one at a time, match when the contract is worth having and fits what they'll pay to keep a young player (their re-signing budget, or up to 5% past the tax), and come to terms with their own after ten days without a sheet. Top free agents who are restricted don't take bids: offer sheets replace them.
- **Waivers and buyouts**: a player released during the regular season with guaranteed money left spends two days on waivers; teams claim in reverse order of record (the AI when the contract is worth having and it has a roster spot and the cap room, or it's a minimum deal), a claim takes over the contract and takes the dead money off the old team's books, and you can claim from the Free Agents screen. Claims close with the regular season, as signings do: anyone still on waivers then clears. Preseason cuts, minimum camp deals and buyouts skip waivers. Veterans (28+) can be bought out from the Release confirmation: they give back up to 22% of what they're owed (more when older or on a team that isn't contending), which comes off the dead money. The day after the trade deadline, AI teams out of contention with a full roster buy out a role veteran or two (30 or older, his deal up this season or next, outside the team's six best, under 82), and each signs for the minimum with the contender he likes best: about five a season. Released players, bought out or waived, can't re-sign with the team that let them go that season, AI fills included.

**NBA Cup** (`src/core/league/cup.ts`): each season the teams are drawn into six groups of five from pots by last season's record, and each team plays four group games on Cup nights in November (Tuesdays and Fridays, the last one the day after Thanksgiving). The three group winners and the best runner-up in each conference reach the December knockouts: quarterfinals and semifinals at the better seed, then a championship on a neutral floor. Group, quarterfinal and semifinal games count in the standings; the championship counts in neither the standings nor season stats. As in the NBA, 80 games per team are scheduled up front with the knockout window kept clear, and once the groups are decided the teams that didn't advance (and quarterfinal losers) get their last games there, so everyone plays 82. Group ties break on head-to-head, point differential and points scored in group games, then last season's record. The League hub has a Cup tab (group tables, bracket, championship); the winner, runner-up and Cup MVP go into awards and history.

**Trading** (`src/core/league/trades.ts`, `proposals.ts`, `deadline.ts`): the trade dock gives the other GM's two main reasons under the value meter (a veteran worth less to a rebuilder, picks that count extra for a rebuilder or don't help a contender win now, salary that puts them into the tax, players they'd have to waive, depth that doesn't add up to talent, an asset that changed hands this season counting at face value, the two-firsts limit), and warns you when you'd give up far more league-neutral value than you get even if they say yes. When they turn a proposal down, a sheet offers up to three versions they would sign, best for you first: something more from you, one of their pieces left out, or one of their bad contracts for you to absorb. The Find tab lists every player AI teams can trade, filtered by position, OVR, age and the salary you can take back, with each player's value tier (Franchise, All-Star, Starter, Rotation, Depth, or Negative for a contract worth less than nothing) and whether his team looks ready to move him or builds around him; tapping one opens the builder with their ask. AI teams also call with real offers: a team that wants one of your players sends its best package for him, a rebuilder offers a veteran, a taxpayer attaches picks to a contract it wants off its books when free agency opens and you have room, and on draft night teams offer to move up into your pick. Offers come about once every ten days in season and every third day before the deadline, at most four at once and one per team, each standing five to seven days, or until you've seen it (an unread offer stands up to three weeks, so a long sim doesn't let it lapse unseen; the deadline-day mail stops a sim while offers wait), with no player in two open offers at once and the pitch worded from the team's record and its thin spot; each is a deal the team accepts and the rules allow, never more than 10% against you in neutral value, accepted from the inbox or the Trade screen's Offers tab (or countered in the builder), and drawn on its own random stream so it never changes a game. Players you mark untouchable are never asked for, in offers or in "what would make this work?". AI-to-AI deals (`aiTrades.ts`) keep the NBA's calendar: a trickle before December 15 (summer signings can't move until then), more into January and again from mid-January, about two a day in the deadline's last week and a rush on deadline day. Most are sales: a rebuilder's veterans, a retooling team's older players and a star on an expiring deal who won't extend are shopped (the seller's best three, to every contender, and to retooling teams for a 76+ player 30 or younger) and the deal the seller likes best is taken, up to four players a side, so two- and three-for-one consolidations happen. The rest are salary dumps, picks attached, by teams over the tax line; a team out of contention over it, or a contender past the second apron, also tries on the summer market's third day, in the preseason, a week before the deadline and on deadline day. An AI team a deal takes past 15 waives the end of its bench (up to three, the cheapest to let go first, dead money counted). Teams trade on draft night (moving up into a first, buying a second), over the summer from the market's third day and in the preseason. Over four seasons on three seeds (42, 7, 99): about 40 AI-to-AI trades a season (32-46), 30 of them in the regular season (46% in its last week) and 11 on draft night, in the summer and in the preseason, with a player rated 87+ moving about once a season. Weekly rumors run in the last three weeks, with a deadline-day briefing and a recap of the season's trades the day after. With "Stop before every pick" the draft waits before each team's pick, so you can trade for the pick on the clock or take offers for yours.

**Deal terms** (`src/ui/trade/Terms.tsx`): a team can protect its own first as it trades it (top 4, 8, 10, 14 or 20: if it lands there it stays home and nothing more is owed; a pick already protected, or another team's, keeps its terms), give another team the right to swap firsts in a future draft (both must hold their own unprotected firsts of that draft; the right rides on the taker's first, goes with it if it's traded, and is settled when the lottery sets the order: if the other first landed higher the two change hands, in the news and the transaction log), and send cash (each team sends and takes up to about 5% of the cap a season, $8.3M in 2026-27; cash isn't salary, comes off the sender's profit and onto the receiver's, outside revenue sharing). AI GMs value a protected pick by the slots it still conveys in, a swap right by its expected gain over both picks' projected slots through their pick lens, and cash at about what second-round picks sell for ($2-3M buys a typical one; rebuilders a little more, contenders less). Labels name the terms everywhere ("2028 1st (NYK) · top-10 prot.", "2029 1st · ATL may swap"), the deal card and dock say what each does ("stays yours about 23% of the time; ATL count it 41% lower"), "What would make this work?" closes a small gap with a little cash before a pick or a player, and a turned-down deal can come back without your protection or with cash added. AI teams never use these terms.

**Transaction log** (`src/core/league/transactions.ts`): every trade (what each side sent), signing and re-signing, extension, release and waiver, claim, buyout, two-way deal and conversion, renounced rights, option decision (yours, and the player opt-outs that make the news), qualifying offer (extended, withdrawn or taken), offer sheet and match, exercised pick swap and draft pick (the first round and yours) is logged with the player's name and the pick labels as they were that day, so it reads the same after he retires or the pick is made. AI teams' quiet roster fills and minimum camp deals aren't listed. Trades and every move of your team stay for good, even after you move to another job; other teams' moves are dropped after two seasons (about 19 KB a season, 55 KB in all). A trade line names swap rights and cash ("for a 2028 1st · top-10 prot., the right to swap 2029 firsts and $1.0M in cash"). Each team page has a Moves tab (by season, the summer as its own block, filtered by trades, signings, releases or other), a player's page has a Transactions card ("Acquired by NYK from BOS with a 2027 1st for …", "Extended by BOS: 4 yrs / $112.0M"), and the Trade screen's partner card lists your last three deals with that team.

**God mode** (`src/core/league/godMode.ts`, `/god`): an in-game editor, off by default and turned on or off at any time (Settings → God mode, or the hub's switch); edits made stay when it's off. While it's on, a wand in the top bar opens the hub, every player's and team's page has Edit, and the Trade screen has Force. The player editor sets his ratings (each on the 40-99 scale, or all his skills at once to a chosen OVR, with the OVR, potential and archetype they'd give him shown as you go), development, bio (name, position, number, age, height with his size rating, weight, country, college), health (an injury and its games out, healing, wear, injury proneness), team (another team takes his contract with him as in a trade; free agency leaves no dead money; a free agent who joins a team signs his asking deal) and contract (this contract season's salary and length with the usual raises, an option, a two-way deal). The team editor sets its city, name, abbreviation and colors (its mark and its players' avatars follow), front-office strategy, cash, hype and its head coach's development and tactics ratings (the coach registry follows), and heals and rests everyone at a tap. New players are made to order (name or a random one, position, age, height, OVR, development) as free agents or onto a team, on a random stream of their own; Force puts a deal through whatever the other GM, the cap, the roster limits or the calendar say. An edit is checked whole before anything changes and applies whole or not at all: rules it bends are listed as warnings (a 16th player, a fourth two-way), and only what would break the league stops it (a team left with fewer than five players to dress in season, a duplicate abbreviation, a number a teammate wears or the team has retired, a prospect moved off the draft board, a pick swap on picks that aren't both teams' own open firsts). OVR, potential, archetype, tendencies, rotations, roles and jersey numbers follow at once; edits wait while a sim runs or a live game is in progress (it replays from its journal with the rosters it tipped off with), and Settings counts the league's edits (`League.godEdits`). The editor's sheets load the first time one opens, so with god mode off it costs the game nothing.

AI front offices pick a strategy by where their roster ranks (about ten contenders, ten rebuilders) and blend in their record as the season goes, so a contender at 10-30 starts selling before the deadline. They re-sign within a budget that fits that strategy (a young player worth 80+, 28 or under, can be kept up to the tax line, a contender's to the first apron, and any 80+ player up to 4% of the cap past it), bring back any of their own free agents on Bird rights once the market opens, give contenders the first look at the market each day, shop their veterans when rebuilding (26-27-year-olds too, all but their best) or fading (from 28), add their third-best player to land an 85+ star, qualify their young players and match offer sheets by value and budget, trade among themselves a couple of times a season (both sides have to like the deal, and nobody sends away more than two firsts in one trade), value consolidation and salary dumps honestly, put second-round picks on two-way deals, bring a clearly better free agent into summer camp even with a full roster, and make preseason cuts by value. A rebuilding team near the bottom of its conference turns to its youth (`core/ai/coachAI.ts`): from about its 20th game for a bottom-four rebuilder, after the deadline for any rebuilder 11th or lower, and halfway with 15 games left for any non-contender 12th or lower, its rotation puts players 22 and under first, plays its veterans less and rests them more, and two-way players get minutes. It costs such a team 2 to 3 points per 100, as tanking does, gives the league about three sub-25-win teams a season (one and a half before) and keeps the win spread near 12 over twelve seasons; the news says so once a season, and the user's team never does it. On casual difficulty their trade discount applies to your own players and picks, not ones you just traded for, so a chain of trades can't compound it. Their strategy lens only adds a premium for what they take in (for a bad contract coming in, at most 15% off its burden): an AI team never sells you its own players or picks below their neutral value, and anything that changed hands this season is valued at face value, so flipping a veteran from a rebuilder to a contender isn't free money. A pick's value moves smoothly toward its draft, and the pick on the clock is worth the prospect it will become. "What would make this work?" adds picks and players together, sends a player when your roster is full, and says whether salary or value was the problem.

## Design

The redesign's rule is coherence: one obvious place for everything, at full depth, with the detail one tap down instead of behind a mode. `src/ui/DESIGN.md` is the contract (the information architecture, how screens change the league, components, copy).

- **Five tabs by role**:
  - **Today**: what needs you now (the agenda, the staff's top advice, the next game, your corner of the standings, your last games, new mail), the inbox, settings.
  - **Team**: who plays and how, under one team header (Roster with its players, ratings, stats and contracts views; Rotation; Tactics; Lineups), and your players' pages.
  - **Games**: your schedule as the season's spine, one matchup page per opponent (`/games/:tid`: Preview on game day with the staff's briefing, Scout, Plan with the staff's counters, and Matchups), the live game and its report.
  - **League**: everyone else (standings with the odds, playoffs, the Cup, stats, scores, awards, news, history at `/league/history`), and other teams' pages (`/teams/:tid`, each with a Matchup button instead of its own scouting).
  - **Office**: the GM's desk (the phase, one cap sheet, and desks for Trade, Free agents, Re-sign, Draft, Picks & moves, Finances, the Owner and your Career).
  - Every route from before the redesign redirects to its new place (`src/ui/routes/legacy.tsx`).
- **The Continue bar** names the next step ("Sim to next game", "Watch & coach", "Start regular season") and runs it; its arrow opens the Play options, and while a sim runs it becomes the progress bar with Stop. "Quick sim this game" plays it and toasts the result with a link to its report, without leaving the screen you're on.
- **The agenda** (`src/core/league/agenda.ts`): the decisions waiting on you, read from the league as it stands. Required ones (a roster over or under its opening-night limits, an offer sheet on your restricted free agent, your pick on the clock, a new job after a firing) take the Continue bar's place and open in a sheet, each with the staff's call one tap away ("Cut 1 player · Release Bronny James"); choices that can't be undone and cost something take a second tap. Optional ones (trade offers, 10-day contracts running out, players who want out, job offers, the owner's ultimatum) never block. A new league runs its first week as agenda items (your rotation, a playbook, scouting the opener, coaching a game live), and Settings brings the guide back.
- **One voice for advice** (`src/core/ai/assistant.ts`): everything the staff says about your team, your next opponent, your roster spots and contracts and the owner's mood is a card of one shape tagged with its area, and where the staff can make the change (a playbook, a coverage, rest on back-to-backs) it does it in one tap. Today shows the most urgent; each screen shows its own.
- **Built for players who know sims**: understandable means fast scanning, no decoding and no hunting. Every figure says what it is and which way is good, the same thing has one name and one format everywhere (the vocabulary table in `src/ui/DESIGN.md`: 3P, ORtg/DRtg/Net, "out 8 games", "thru 28-29 PO", "7th of 30", "34-90", POT…), money is written by one module (`src/core/format.ts`), and colour that means "how good" has one order everywhere (the OVR tiers, with a key under every list of badges). Screens are dense by default: spacing comes from shared tokens and list rows are 48px.
- **Search** (`src/ui/components/QuickSearch.tsx`, `src/ui/search.ts`): the top bar's search, or Ctrl/⌘ K or "/", finds any player (on a team, a free agent, a prospect, retired), team (with its matchup page beside it), screen by the words players use for it ("cap" finds the Office, "depth chart" the rotation) or glossary word. Names match without accents and in any order ("jal wil").
- **The glossary** (`src/ui/glossary.ts`): the game's jargon (drop, ice, MLE, Bird rights, eFG%...) in plain words, from Today or Settings, and dotted words on screens open their entry. **Teaching** (Settings → Help, and New League) turns off the first-week guide, its welcome mail and Today's Glossary link in one switch; New League remembers the choice on this device.
- **Courtside, decluttered**: the dark broadcast look (Archivo, the brand orange on controls, team colours on identity surfaces, number-plate slabs) with less on screen: one slab per screen, hairline lists over boxed cards, scoreboard figures, a headline and one line per card, and explanations behind a "?" (toggles included). Phone first: wide screens get the same column, centred.
- **Big moments** (`src/ui/moments.ts`, Settings → Big moments): a playoff or play-in spot clinched, a series won, a title, the NBA Cup, your draft pick, a free agent of 80+ OVR signed, or an award for one of your players or you takes the screen for a moment in your colours (confetti for a title or the Cup), once, after a sim finishes and outside the live game. Whatever is already true when a league opens counts as seen. Key figures count up as a screen opens, and every animation respects reduced motion.
- **Identity**: the icon is the coach's chalk, an X and a play swept into the ball (`public/icon.svg`; `scripts/qa/icons.mjs` and `scripts/qa/native-assets.mjs` render the PWA, Android and iOS icons and splash screens from it). The start screen leads with the league you played last, as a slab in its team's colours.

stats.nba.com, ESPN and Basketball Reference block many cloud IPs, so everything except the Basketball Reference pages comes from GitHub-hosted mirrors or NBA.com's S3 bucket, which download from anywhere.

EPM exports need a Dunks & Threes subscription, so nothing downloads them: the three regular-season CSVs are committed in `data/epm/` (`scripts/data/epm.ts` reads every `.csv` there, taking the season from its rows, so a new season's export can be dropped in under any name; a season exported twice keeps the later file by name). Players are matched by the NBA.com id the play-by-play crosswalk already has (all 725 of them), else by name and team. Without the folder the build falls back to box plus-minus. The committed seed took EPM with `npm run data:epm` rather than a rebuild: calibration never touches the impact ratings, so each player's moved by what EPM changes in them as derived (Jokić's defensive IQ, carried by his box-score defense, fell 12; Capela's rose 7), the rest of the seed stayed as calibrated, and each real season got its EPM. A full rebuild (`data:build`, or the workflow) takes EPM in on its own. EPM was later given a larger part (step 3 below), and the committed seed took that the same way, with `npm run data:epm-reweight`: each impact rating is a base plus a scale times its terms and EPM's term is a weight times a regressed z-score, so each player moved by exactly what the new weights and scales change in his ratings as derived, his z-scores taken as the build takes them (three-season EPM from the CSVs, against the same population, regressed by the same minutes, read from the seed's own lines). 484 players moved; Wembanyama's perimeter defense rose 14, and the biggest OVR moves were a few points (Sochan 62 → 67, Embiid 88 → 90; Jericho Sims 67 → 63). The 60 players it doesn't move (rookies and others without an NBA sample or EPM) are leveled back to the OVR they had, as the build levels them to their draft slot and reputation. A rebuild would also recalibrate the shooting ratings around the new impact ratings; that isn't redone. The seed's source records the blend, so the script applies once, and it refuses a seed whose EPM seasons no longer match the exports.

The **projection** is Dunks & Threes' estimate of each player's 2026-27 season: three seasons of EPM and box score regressed by sample, aged a year, and the rookies' college numbers translated. The seed's ratings describe players as they are on opening night, and that is exactly what it estimates, so it is the seed's last and heaviest word (`scripts/data/epm-projection.ts`, run on a finished seed by `data:build` or on its own with `npm run data:epm-projection`). Each player it lists (466 of the seed's players; matched by NBA.com id, else the play-by-play crosswalk's name matching) moves in five steps. His block and rebound multipliers, usage, three-point share and passing tendencies move by his projected rate over his three-season one, and his shooting ratings by what the change in his percentages is worth across the seed. His offensive IQ moves by the change from three-season to projected offensive EPM, and defensive IQ and perimeter and interior defense by the defensive half, each at what a standard deviation of EPM is worth in that rating (about 10 points, 6 in perimeter defense). His OVR is then set 80% by where his projected EPM ranks: the seed's own raw OVRs, sorted, are handed out in projected-EPM order, so the league keeps its spread of ratings (and the display scale, the fictional leagues, development and aging their tuning). The move goes into the feel, creation and defense ratings that OVR weighs, at the end of the floor where the projection disagrees with his ratings, and not into steals, blocks, boards or free throws, which the projected box line has set. Stamina follows half the change in his minutes. Last, every rating and multiplier goes back to its old mean over the rotations, since the projection ages every veteran a year but the league they make up is no worse. A rookie, with no real line to compare, moves 60% of the way to what his projection reads as. Players it doesn't list (46 rostered, waived or abroad by Dunks & Threes' rosters) keep their OVR. OVR now follows projected EPM at r 0.99 over the 300-odd players projected for 15+ minutes (0.86 before), and over six `real-check` seasons each team's net rating follows its players' projected EPM at r 0.93 (0.89) with a slope of 1.0, while every league rate stays within 3% of where it was, the win spread is 13.6 (13.0; the NBA's is about 13) and the net-rating spread 6.1 (5.5). The simulated box lines follow the projected ones as well or better (rebounds r 0.94, assists 0.90, free-throw percentage 0.92, minutes 0.85; turnovers stay the hardest, about 0.48). 410 rostered players' OVRs moved, 69 by 5 or more: Curry 89 → 92, Jarrett Allen 84 → 90, Ausar Thompson 81 → 87, Harden 89 → 84, LeBron 86 → 83, and the top rookies down to where a rookie's projection sits (Dybantsa 80 → 76, Peterson and Boozer 79 → 74). Each player keeps his projection, which his Stats tab shows during that season, and the seed's source notes it, so a seed takes it once. Seven players are projected with teams the seed doesn't have them on (six free agents since signed, and Prosper in Memphis); rosters aren't changed.

What's real on opening night, besides the rosters and ratings:

- **Schedule**: the NBA's 2026-27 calendar, from opening night (Oct 20: BOS@DET, PHI@NYK, OKC@SAS) to Apr 11, with the real All-Star break (the trade deadline is the Thursday ten days before the All-Star Game, as in generated seasons). Later seasons, and fictional leagues, use the generated schedule.
- **NBA Cup**: the real groups (East A: BKN, DET, MIL, ORL, TOR …) and their 60 group games on the real Cup nights (Oct 30 to Nov 27), with quarterfinals Dec 4-5, semifinals Dec 8 and the championship Dec 11. The knockout matchups and the two games for each team that doesn't reach them are decided by the simulated group stage, as the NBA will do. If a seed has no Cup data, the missing games go on open December dates instead and there is no Cup that season.
- **Draft picks**: RealGM's ownership rows for 2027-2033 (`scripts/data/extra/picks.ts`). Each pick gets exactly one owner. Pools ("less favorable of MIL and NOP") and swap rights are resolved on a projected draft order (team strength from the seed's ratings, regressed toward the middle for later years), and conditions that depend on other picks ("if DAL conveys its 2027 first", "if not already settled") are checked against picks already resolved. A pick that conveys only outside the top N ("3-30 to Charlotte") is stored as a top-N protected pick. Where several outcomes are possible, the most likely owner gets the pick. The build prints every team's first-rounders and compares them with LD Sport's table.
- **Injuries**: players listed out on opening night start injured. The time out is counted in their team's real games before the expected return date, and a return after the regular season costs playoff games too. ESPN's description is mapped to the game's injury types where one fits (Torn ACL, Hamstring Strain...), otherwise kept descriptive (Torn Labrum). Injuries are off when the league's injury setting is off.
- **Two-way contracts** are marked and don't count toward the 15-man roster.
- **Positions** come from each player's box-score position in his latest season (Basketball Reference's current roster pages only say G / F / C). Rookies without one get theirs from the G/F/C label, size and skills: a guard who handles and passes like a lead guard for his size is a PG, a forward who handles more and rebounds less is an SF.

How ratings are made (`scripts/data/`):

1. **Play-by-play profiles** (`nba/pbp.ts`): one pass over each season builds every player's shot diet by zone (the engine's zones: rim, paint, mid-range, corner three, above-the-break three) with make rates, how he creates (pull-ups, drives, cuts, post moves, putbacks, transition, share of makes assisted), assists by the zone they set up, turnover and foul types, fouls drawn, true rebound rates from on-court opportunities, and rim deterrence (opponents' rim attempts and rim FG% with him on the floor). It also rebuilds every lineup stint.
2. **RAPM** (`nba/rapm.ts`): ridge-regressed adjusted plus-minus over about 200,000 stints from three seasons (recency-weighted, home court modeled), split into offense and defense. Matchup data (`nba/matchups.ts`) adds points saved per 100 possessions guarded, relative to what the same scorers did against everyone else.
3. **Attributes** (`derive.ts`): seasons are weighted by recency and minutes (2025-26 ×1.0, 2024-25 ×0.6, 2023-24 ×0.3), with Bayesian priors on percentages. Finishing, floaters and mid-range come from zone make rates; handling from self-creation and lost-ball rate; passing from assist and bad-pass rates; rebounding from true rebound rates; defense from RAPM, matchup defense, rim deterrence, steals and blocks. Dunks & Threes EPM (box plus-minus done better: a box and tracking prior refined by lineup data) is the largest single input to offensive and defensive IQ, at 10 rating points per standard deviation of three-season EPM (regressed by sample size) beside RAPM's 6 and 7, and its defensive half joins RAPM in perimeter and interior defense at 5; every term of a rating EPM is in is then scaled down (to 0.72 in the IQs, 0.8 and 0.84 in the defenses: `EPM_BLEND`) so each rating keeps its spread, which leaves EPM about half of each IQ, up from about a third. A season EPM doesn't cover keeps box plus-minus at its old weight, and without play-by-play EPM carries all four impact ratings alone. Over 12 `real-check` seasons on the same rng seeds, this blend made simulated team net ratings follow the players' real EPM at r 0.86 (0.84 at EPM's first weight) and their real box plus-minus at r 0.88 (0.85), widened the spread of wins toward the NBA's (win SD 11.4, from 10.5; the NBA's is about 12), and left every league rate and the players' simulated lines where they were. Not scaling the other terms down (EPM simply added) tracks real impact a little better still (r 0.90) but widens the real league's raw ratings by about a quarter, which the fictional leagues' spread, development and aging are tuned to: a change for the engine, not the seed. An earlier engine kept EPM at box plus-minus's weight, because a harder lean that also took over most of RAPM's weight made simulated team results track the players' real EPM worse (r 0.77, from 0.80).
4. **Engine-in-the-loop calibration** (`calibrate.ts`): seasons are simulated repeatedly and each player's simulated profile is compared with his real one. Shooting ratings and hidden per-player tendencies are nudged until he reproduces it. The tendencies cover usage, three-point rate, rim, mid-range and corner-three shares, self-creation, passing, turnovers, share of rebounds, steals, blocks and fouls. The tendencies absorb role and opportunity, so they don't distort the visible ratings. To keep them from overfitting one context, half the calibration games use next season's rosters and half the 2025-26 teams the stats came from, each game draws the coach's playbook from his top three, and players randomly miss games so rotations get re-planned around them.
5. **Rookies and small samples**: 2026 draftees are shaped by their college production (shooter, passer, rim protector...) and leveled to their draft slot and reputation. Short NBA samples are shrunk toward the level their minutes suggest; calibration then settles again.
6. **OVR** is a fitted, size-aware blend of attributes. The weights were fitted so that real players' ratings predict half regressed box plus-minus and half RAPM, plus the minutes and usage coaches gave them (OVR follows three-season EPM at r 0.94 over the 373 players with 1,500+ minutes, from 0.92 at EPM's first weight; with that first weight, OVR vs RAPM was r 0.78 and vs BPM 0.85, and with box plus-minus in EPM's place 0.76 and 0.88).
7. **Projection** (`epm-projection.ts`): Dunks & Threes' 2026-27 EPM projection then has the last word: box line, impact ratings and 80% of each player's OVR level (see Real data above).

`npm run real-check` simulates a season with the real league and compares every regular with his real 2025-26 numbers. With the current seed, sim-vs-real correlations over about 225 regulars are 0.91 for points, 0.90 for assists, 0.93 for rebounds, 0.92 for blocks, 0.88 for threes made, 0.81 for steals, 0.82 for minutes per game and 0.69 for true shooting, and each team's net rating follows its players' real EPM at r 0.86 and their real box plus-minus at r 0.88 (averages over 12 seeds; 0.84 and 0.85 at EPM's first weight). Minutes follow because coaches remember last season's roles, counted once and converted to full-strength minutes, so real rotations carry over without burying healthy veterans. Every league per-game rate (pace, points, threes, free throws, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, turnovers, fouls) lands within 5% of the 2025-26 NBA, and shot diets match too: about 0.93 for rim share, 0.88 for mid-range share and 0.93 for three-point share. It also prints the game shape (margin spread, blowouts, overtime, win spread), each team's net rating against its players' real box plus-minus and EPM (and their projected EPM, for a seed built with it), the leaders, single-game highs and the DPOY ladder, so outliers are easy to spot.

## Ship to phones

- **PWA**: deploy `dist/` to any static host and use "Add to Home Screen". It works offline after the first load, and saves stay on the device (IndexedDB, with persistent storage requested). Saves from before the redesign (save format 4) can't be opened: the main menu lists them to export or delete. Saves are incremental (game results live in their own store and only new ones are written; older ratings snapshots keep just OVR and potential, so a long career grows by about 0.4 MB a season), a live-game journal that's damaged or isn't for the game still to be played today is dropped on load, a failed save says so, offers a backup and keeps retrying (a brand-new league included), opening another league, importing a backup or starting a new one saves the open league first (and offers an export when it can't), and a league open in two windows can't overwrite itself. A new version downloads in the background and applies on the next launch, never mid-game; reloading after an update or from the error screen saves the league first.
- **Backups**: Settings → Export backup saves the league as a JSON file (the share sheet in the app, a download on the web). Import it from Settings or the start screen on any device.
- **Android**: the _Android APK_ workflow builds a debug APK on every push to `main` (download it from the run's artifacts). Pushing a `v*` tag attaches the APK to a release. Locally: `npm run cap:sync && npx cap open android`. The native shell adds the hardware Back button (closes the topmost sheet first, goes Home when there is no earlier screen, saves before leaving), haptics, edge-to-edge insets and no service worker (the files ship in the APK).
- **Android signing**: Android only installs an update signed with the same key as the installed app. Without signing secrets, each workflow run signs with a throwaway debug key, so a new build only installs after uninstalling the old one, which deletes its saves (export a backup first). To sign every build with one key, create a keystore once and keep it safe (a lost key means users must reinstall):
  ```bash
  keytool -genkeypair -v -keystore release.keystore -alias hoops -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000
  base64 -w0 release.keystore   # paste the output into ANDROID_KEYSTORE_BASE64
  ```
  Then add four repository secrets (Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions): `ANDROID_KEYSTORE_BASE64`, `ANDROID_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD`, `ANDROID_KEY_ALIAS` (`hoops` above) and `ANDROID_KEY_PASSWORD`. Debug APKs are then signed with that key, and `v*` tags also get a signed release APK (`app-release.apk`) on the release. The version code is the workflow's run number, so each build counts as newer than the last.
- **iOS**: `npm run cap:sync && npx cap open ios` on a Mac with Xcode.
- **GitHub Pages**: the _Deploy PWA to Pages_ workflow publishes every push to `main` (and runs by hand from the Actions tab), once Settings → Pages → Source is set to "GitHub Actions". It builds for the site's own path (`/<repo>/` for a project site, `/` for a user site or a custom domain). Pages on a private repo needs a paid GitHub plan.

## Design decisions

- Ratings are shown on a 2K-style 40-99 scale, fully visible. OVR is anchored to the league's distribution, so 93+ is a superstar, 89+ All-NBA, 87+ an All-Star, 82+ a quality starter, 78+ a starter, 72+ a rotation player and 67+ a bench player, with about 74 the median rostered player. Each tier has one badge colour (gold, purple, blue, green, light grey, grey) and every list of badges carries the key; attribute ratings and skill percentiles use the same colour order. Attributes use the same 40-99 scale (league median ≈ 68).
- The contracts table includes camp deals, so some teams start with 16-20 players. AI teams make preseason cuts down to 15 standard contracts at league creation (two-way contracts don't count). If your team is over, the Assistant GM suggests who to release before opening night.
- Difficulty is one of three presets (`src/core/league/difficulty.ts`), Normal by default: Easy takes deals about 12% under value and has your players ask 10% less, with no opposing game plans and a patient owner; Hard wants a 12% premium, asks 12% more, has every coach game-plan you and an impatient owner. Settings shows them side by side.
- Future picks start with their real owners (2027-2033). The game's pick model is one owner per pick plus an optional top-N protection and swap right, so RealGM's conditions are simplified (seed swaps are still resolved on the projected order): swaps and "more/less favorable of" pools go to the projected outcome, protections that send the pick to a third team, or roll over to a later year, are approximated as "stays with the original team that year, obligation ends", and picks the NBA has forfeited stay with their original team.
- The real schedule is used for 2026-27 only. Games the NBA hasn't published yet are filled on open December dates.
- Team logos are original monograms in each franchise's colors (no trademarked artwork).
